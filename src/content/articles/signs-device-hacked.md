# Signs Your Phone or Computer Has Been Hacked

It's 2 a.m. and you're convinced someone is in your phone. The battery's dying in hours, an app you don't remember installing is on your home screen, and your cousin just asked why you sent them that weird message. Panic is natural. Before you throw the device in the river, though, know this: most "signs of hacking" turn out to have a boring explanation.

That's not a reason to ignore the signs — it's a reason to look at them honestly, group them, and act when several appear together. Here's what to actually watch for and what to do if you think your device is compromised.

## The signs that mean something

A single oddity is usually nothing. These signs, in combination, are what raise a real red flag.

### Pop-ups and ads appearing out of nowhere

Ads and pop-ups that appear outside your browser — on your home screen or desktop — often mean unwanted software (sometimes called adware) snuck in alongside an app you installed or a page you visited. This is one of the most common genuine malware symptoms.

### A battery that drains for no reason

Malware doing background work burns power. But so do old batteries, bright screens, and apps syncing in the background. Battery drain is a weak signal alone; treat it as important when it arrives with other symptoms.

### Data usage you can't explain

On a phone, check your data usage settings. Malware phoning home uses data. If your usage is far above normal and nothing in your habits changed, that's worth a second look.

### Apps or programs you didn't install

Go through your installed apps and programs. Anything you don't recognize is suspicious by definition. On Android, check for apps with unusually broad permissions; on a computer, look through installed programs for entries you never added.

### Accounts doing things without you

This one isn't a guess — it's proof. If messages are sent from your accounts, emails leave your outbox, or your friends report weird links from you, an attacker has reached your accounts, not just your device. That usually means a stolen password or session, which is a different problem than malware on the device itself.

### Your device is slow or hot

Malware chewing through CPU makes a device sluggish and warm. But an old, storage-full device does the same thing. If your phone is suddenly slow for no reason, our [guide to fixing a slow Android phone](/articles/android-phone-running-slow) and similar Windows fixes are worth a read before you suspect the worst.

### You're suddenly locked out of accounts

You can't get into your email or social accounts, or your password "stopped working" even though you're sure you typed it right. That's not malware on your device — it's an attacker changing your password on a service, which usually means your account (not your phone or computer) was compromised. Treat it as its own emergency and secure the account through the service's recovery flow right away.

## The honest caveat

Here's the uncomfortable part: virtually every sign above has a boring cause. Battery drain? Probably just your screen. Slow device? Storage nearly full. Strange pop-ups? A sketchy site you visited once. A quick, honest check of the boring causes first will save you hours of panic — and it's the right order of operations even if something is wrong.

There's also a psychological trap worth naming: when people fear they've been hacked, they start seeing evidence everywhere. A few extra seconds of loading time becomes "the hacker's doing." Two pop-ups become a campaign. If you catch yourself doing that, pause and get specific — write down the three concrete things that worried you, and judge those three rather than the general dread. Usually one has an obvious explanation.

## What to do if you're genuinely worried

If several signs show up together, or you have hard evidence like messages sent from your account, work this checklist from most important to least.

### 1. Secure your accounts from a different device

If your device is compromised, act from a device you trust. Change the passwords on your important accounts — email first, since it's the reset key for everything else — and turn on two-factor authentication. A [password manager](/articles/password-manager-guide) makes fresh, unique passwords quick to create.

### 2. Check your sign-in history

Most major services show recent logins and active sessions. Look for logins from locations or devices you don't recognize, and sign out of anything unfamiliar. If you find some, that confirms account compromise and tells you which passwords to change first.

### 3. Update the device

Install pending system and app updates. Security updates fix the known holes attackers use; if the malware rode in on an old vulnerability, updating often closes the door.

### 4. Remove what you don't recognize

Uninstall the mystery apps and programs. On a phone, also check which apps have unusual permissions (camera, microphone, SMS) and revoke anything that looks off. Then reboot.

### 5. Run a malware scan

Built-in protection — the security app your phone came with, or the antivirus already on your computer — catches most common malware these days. A one-off scan with a reputable second scanner can catch stragglers. Avoid the "system cleaner" apps in ads; those are often the problem, not the solution.

### 6. If it's bad, reset and restore

If problems continue, factory-reset the phone or reinstall the operating system on the computer, then restore from a backup made *before* the problems started. Restoring from a recent-but-infected backup just brings the trouble back.

## Did a scam start this?

A huge share of device infections start with a click — a phishing email, a scam link, or a convincing [AI-generated message](/articles/ai-scams-deepfake-calls). If you suspect that's how it started, the device cleanup is only half the fix; the other half is checking what you handed over (passwords, payment details) and watching your accounts for follow-up scams.

## Wrapping up

Most hacking scares are false alarms, but real compromises share a pattern: pop-ups, unexplained battery drain and data use, unknown apps, and accounts acting up — usually several at once. Check the boring causes first, and if the signs persist, secure your accounts from another device, clean up, and reset if you have to. Calm, methodical action beats panic every time.
