The difference between a meh answer and a genuinely useful one from ChatGPT usually isn't the model — it's the prompt. Vague questions get vague answers. A clear, specific prompt gets a clear, specific result.

This guide teaches one simple formula you can reuse for almost anything, shows weak-versus-strong prompts side by side, and covers the mistakes that quietly sabotage most prompts.

## The one formula that fixes most prompts

Think of a good prompt as containing four ingredients:

1. **Role** — who should ChatGPT pretend to be?
2. **Context** — what does it need to know to help you?
3. **Task** — what exactly do you want it to do?
4. **Format** — how should the answer look?

Role + context + task + format. That's it.

**Weak prompt:** "Write an email."

**Strong prompt:** "You are a friendly customer-support writer. I run a small online store and a customer's order arrived a week late. Please write a 3-sentence apology email to them, offer a 10% discount code, and sound sincere but not robotic."

The second version works because it tells ChatGPT *who* to be, *what situation* it's helping with, *what* to produce, and *how* the output should look.

## Before and after examples

**Example 1 — Planning a trip**

Weak: "Plan a trip to Italy."

Strong: "Act as a travel planner. I have 5 days in Italy in September, a budget of around $2,500, I like food and history, and I hate crowds. Give me a day-by-day itinerary with morning, afternoon, and evening plans, plus one backup idea for rainy days. Use a table."

**Example 2 — Work writing**

Weak: "Help me with my resume."

Strong: "Act as a career coach. I've been a customer-service manager for 4 years and want to move into operations. Rewrite this job-description bullet so it sounds more action-oriented, keeps to one line, and starts with a strong verb: [paste the bullet]."

**Example 3 — Learning something**

Weak: "Explain neural networks."

Strong: "Explain how neural networks work to a 15-year-old who knows basic math. Use an analogy from cooking. Keep it under 250 words, avoid jargon, and end with a one-sentence summary."

**Example 4 — Everyday problem-solving**

Weak: "My phone is slow."

Strong: "Act as a tech-support agent for a non-technical person. My Android phone has been sluggish for a week, especially when opening the camera. Suggest the 3 most likely causes and the easiest fixes first, in plain language, under 150 words."

Notice the pattern in every strong example: they all tell the model who to be, what situation to picture, exactly what to produce, and how the output should be shaped. That consistency is what makes the formula repeatable — once it clicks, you'll write better prompts without thinking about the steps.

## Six mistakes that sabotage prompts

**1. Being too vague.** "Make it better" means nothing. Say what "better" looks like.

**2. Asking for everything at once.** ChatGPT works better step by step. "Here's a draft, tighten it" beats "write it, then tighten it, then translate it, then summarize it."

**3. Not giving constraints.** Length, tone, audience, format — if you don't specify, the model guesses. It usually guesses "medium-length, neutral tone."

**4. Not giving it context.** The model only knows what you tell it. Paste the document, the email thread, the code, or the requirements.

**5. Accepting the first answer.** Ask for a revision: "That's too formal, try again with shorter sentences." Iterating is normal, not a failure.

**6. Giving up after one bad prompt.** If the answer is off, tighten the prompt rather than restarting. Small tweaks change results dramatically.

## When to push further: follow-up prompting

Most people stop too early. ChatGPT shines in back-and-forth:

- "Good, but make it 200 words shorter."
- "Add a summary at the top."
- "Now make version 2, but for a beginner audience."
- "Spot any gaps in this plan and list the top three."

Treat the first output as a rough draft. The model is a drafting tool, not a final answer machine. Anything important should be [fact-checked](/articles/ai-hallucinations-fact-check) — including things ChatGPT states with total confidence.

## What ChatGPT is genuinely good at (and not)

**Good at:** drafting emails, summarizing documents, brainstorming, explaining complex topics simply, outlining, rewriting, and turning rough notes into structure.

**Not good at:** up-to-date facts, precise math, or judgment about people and consequences. Always review and verify before acting on anything.

## A starter kit of prompt templates

- **Summarize:** "Summarize this in 5 bullet points, 1 line each: [paste]."
- **Rephrase:** "Rewrite this paragraph for a [junior / senior / non-expert] audience: [paste]."
- **Brainstorm:** "Give me 10 ideas for [topic], with one sentence each. Rank by [how easy / how useful]."
- **Explain simply:** "Explain [topic] like I'm 12, with an analogy."
- **Edit:** "Fix the grammar and tighten this without changing the meaning: [paste]."
- **Plan:** "Break [goal] into steps I can do this week, ordered by effort."
