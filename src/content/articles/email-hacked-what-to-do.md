If someone has hacked your email, take a breath. You can almost always get it back, and the steps below will stop the damage quickly. Do them in this order — each one makes the next harder for the attacker to undo.

Your email is the master key to your digital life, because every account you own is probably attached to that address. That's exactly why email takeovers are such a common attack. Here's the calm, ordered way to respond.

## Signs your email has been hacked

Before the playbook, make sure you're actually dealing with a hack and not a glitch:

- Your contacts mention spam or weird messages from you.
- Your password doesn't work anymore.
- You see emails in your Sent folder you never wrote.
- Your profile name, signature, or reply-to address changed.
- You got a notification about a login from a new device or location you don't recognize.
- A breach-checking service told you your address appeared in a data leak.

Even one of these is enough to act. You don't need to wait for confirmation.

## Step 1: Change your password now

If you can still sign in, that's your first move.

1. Open your email and go to the security or account settings.
2. Choose **Change password**.
3. Pick something long and unique — a random string of 16+ characters, not your pet's name with a number on the end.
4. Do **not** reuse it anywhere else.

If the hacker already changed your password, skip to the recovery flow: click **Forgot password** and use your recovery email, phone number, or backup codes to get back in.

This is exactly the moment a [password manager](/articles/password-manager-guide) pays for itself — it generates and stores a strong password for you so you never have to invent or remember one.

## Step 2: Check your recovery options

An attacker who's in your account will often change the recovery email and phone number, so *you* can't recover it and they can. Fix that immediately:

1. Open the security settings for your account.
2. Look at the **recovery email** and **recovery phone** on file.
3. Remove anything you don't recognize.
4. Make sure your own, current recovery methods are set.

Also add backup codes if your provider offers them, and store them somewhere safe. If a passcode or code ever leaks through a scam, an account protected by a second factor is far harder to take over.

## Step 3: Look for forwarding rules and filters

The quietest way to own an inbox is to read copies of everything without touching anything. Hackers do this with email forwarding rules, so check for them:

1. Go to your mail **settings** (look for "Forwarding and POP/IMAP," "Rules," or "Filters").
2. Turn off any forwarding to an address you don't recognize.
3. Delete any filter that deletes messages or redirects them.

A forwarder means every new email — including the password-reset links from your other accounts — is being copied to the attacker. Removing it is as important as changing the password.

## Step 4: Sign out of every device

Most providers can revoke access on all devices at once. In your security settings, look for something like **Sign out of all sessions** or **Manage devices** and use it. This kicks the hacker off every phone, tablet, and browser where they've signed in, so a stolen session can't survive your password change.

## Step 5: Scan for linked accounts

Go through the places that touch your email:

- **App passwords and connected apps** — revoke any third-party app that has access that you don't recognize or no longer use.
- **Other accounts that use this email** — your banking, shopping, and social logins. If any use the same password you just changed (and if you reuse passwords, some will), change those too.
- **Password reset attempts** — check whether the hacker tried to reset passwords on your other accounts, and change those accounts' passwords if anything looks off.

## Step 6: Turn on two-factor authentication

Once you're back in control, make sure the account is protected with a second login step — a code from an authenticator app, a text message, or a security key. It's the single strongest thing you can do against the next attempt. Our [two-factor authentication guide](/articles/two-factor-authentication-explained) walks through turning it on for email and the other accounts that matter.

## Step 7: Warn your contacts

If the hacker sent spam or scams from your address, people need to know it wasn't you. A short message is enough: "My email was briefly compromised. Ignore anything strange from me in the last few days." If you use a contact group or a social post, even better. Anyone who clicked a link the hacker sent should read up on [AI scam messages](/articles/how-to-identify-ai-scam-message) — many of those come from hijacked accounts.

## If you can't get back in at all

The recovery flow fails most often when the attacker changed your recovery options before you could act. When that happens:

- Use the provider's **account recovery form** — they'll ask questions only you can answer: when the account was created, emails you sent recently, old passwords you remember. Answer honestly and slowly; accuracy matters more than speed.
- **Contact the provider's support team** directly. Most email providers have a dedicated path for compromised accounts, and support agents can verify ownership when the automated flow can't.
- **Don't give up and don't pay anyone.** Legitimate recovery is free. Anyone charging a fee to "get your account back" is running the second half of the scam.

The sooner you start, the better your odds, because a full takeover that goes unnoticed for weeks is much harder to unwind than one caught in the first day.

## Wrapping up

Getting hacked is stressful, but the playbook is simple and you've probably done the hardest step already: you noticed. Change the password, wipe the recovery and forwarding settings, sign out everywhere, scan linked accounts, turn on two-factor authentication, and warn your contacts. Do those in order and you'll shut the attacker out and keep the account yours. More everyday security fixes live in our [troubleshooting guides](/articles/category/troubleshooting).
