A budget laptop is a compromise — but so is every laptop. The trick is knowing which compromises are harmless and which will annoy you every single day.

Here's the honest version: a budget laptop will be slower than a premium one, its screen won't win awards, and its body will be plastic. But for web, email, documents, and streaming, that's genuinely fine for most people. The key is getting the *right* budget laptop, not just the cheapest one.

## Set realistic expectations

At the budget end, manufacturers save money in predictable places. Knowing what to expect keeps you from being disappointed — or fooled by a thin spec sheet.

### Build quality

Expect plastic. That's fine — decent plastic laptops survive years of daily use. What you don't want is a body that visibly flexes when you hold it, or hinges that feel loose. Pick it up in a store if you can, or read reviews that mention build quality.

### Screen

Most budget laptops have a 1080p screen that's perfectly usable indoors. What you'll give up is brightness, color accuracy, and viewing angles. If you'll work near a window, check reviews for brightness complaints before you buy.

### Battery

Budget models vary wildly here. Some cheap laptops get a full workday; others die by lunch. Battery life is one of the hardest things to judge from a spec sheet, so lean on real-world reviews — not the manufacturer's claim — before choosing.

### Performance

A budget laptop won't fly, but for everyday tasks it shouldn't feel sluggish either. The catch is that performance in a cheap laptop is a snapshot: it feels fine on day one and can crawl by year three. That's where the specs below come in.

## The specs that matter most at this level

### RAM: 16 GB if you can, never settle below what you need

This is the single most important decision in a budget laptop, because most budget laptops have RAM soldered in — you can't add more later. 8 GB is still common at the low end and is tight for many people. Spend the extra for 16 GB if the budget allows. Our [RAM buying guide](/articles/how-much-ram-do-you-need) explains the difference in plain terms.

### Storage: an SSD, not a hard drive

At the budget level you might still find laptops with a slow spinning hard drive or a tiny SSD. Never buy the hard-drive version — it'll feel old from the very first boot. And check the SSD's size: a very small drive fills up fast. If a cheap SSD model costs a bit more than a roomy hard-drive model, take the SSD every time.

### CPU: recent generation, mid-range

Look for a recent-generation mid-range chip rather than the oldest or most stripped-down option. You don't need a top-end processor at this price — you need one that isn't already outdated on launch day. Pair it with that 16 GB of RAM and you've solved most of the "slow laptop" problem before it starts.

### Screen: 1080p, and check the panel

Stick to 1080p at minimum. If screen quality matters to you — long reading sessions, photo work — the panel type and brightness matter more than the resolution number.

## What you should expect to give up

- **Dedicated graphics.** Budget laptops run on integrated graphics. That's fine for everything except serious gaming and heavy video editing.
- **Premium materials.** Plastic bodies, thicker bezels, fewer ports. None of this affects what the laptop can actually do.
- **Long update support.** Some budget lines get shorter software support than premium ones, which matters for security. Check the maker's policy if you plan to keep it several years.

## Well-known budget-friendly lines to start from

These aren't recommendations of specific models — those change every few months — but they're the lines where good-value models consistently appear:

- **Acer Aspire and Swift** — dependable everyday machines that regularly appear at budget prices.
- **Lenovo IdeaPad** — strong value, especially for productivity and typing (Lenovo's keyboards are famously good).
- **ASUS VivoBook** — a wide range of thin, light, reasonably priced laptops.
- **HP's budget lines** (Pavilion and below) — broad choice and frequent sales.

Whatever you shortlist, compare it against the [how to choose a laptop guide](/articles/how-to-choose-a-laptop-buying-guide) — that's the master guide to matching specs to your real usage, and it covers what the budget brands won't tell you.

## The two upgrades worth paying for

If you have a little more to spend, put it in this order:

1. **RAM (16 GB).** Non-negotiable for most people, and the top cause of budget laptops feeling slow a year in.
2. **A better screen.** More brightness and a nicer panel transform daily use more than a faster chip does.

Skip the storage upgrade if the model has a card slot or an easily upgraded drive bay, and skip processor upgrades unless you do heavy work — a mid-range chip with 16 GB of RAM is the sweet spot.

## How to buy smart

1. **Set a hard budget** before you look.
2. **Shortlist models from the known budget lines**, not random specs.
3. **Read real-world reviews** for battery life and build — the two things spec sheets lie about most.
4. **Check the RAM and storage** (SSD, not HDD) — the two specs that decide whether it stays fast.
5. **Compare prices across a couple of stores** — budget laptops are discounted constantly, and last year's model at a discount often beats this year's at full price.
6. **Confirm the returns and warranty** before you commit.

## Wrapping up

A budget laptop won't wow you, but the right one will quietly do everything you need for years. Expect plastic, an okay screen, and integrated graphics; demand 16 GB of RAM, an SSD, and a recent mid-range chip. Match that and you've beaten most of the market at the same price.
