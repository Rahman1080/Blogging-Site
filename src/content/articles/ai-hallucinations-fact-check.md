Ask a chatbot a question and it might give you a confident, polished, completely made-up answer. It's not lying — there's no intention behind it. It's what AI researchers call a **hallucination**: a response that sounds plausible but isn't true.

Here's why it happens, the ways it actually bites people, and a practical workflow for fact-checking anything an AI tells you.

## What a hallucination actually is

A hallucination is when an AI generates content that's fabricated but presented with confidence. The model invents facts, cites papers that don't exist, recommends products with made-up names, or asserts a date or law that's wrong — all in flawless, confident language.

Key point: the AI isn't "lying." Lying implies knowing the truth and hiding it. The model has no truth to know.

## Why AI makes things up

It comes down to how these models work. A large language model like ChatGPT is a **word-prediction machine**. When you ask it something, it doesn't look up the answer in a database. It predicts, word by word, the most likely sequence of words based on patterns in its training data.

That means it's not built to be accurate — it's built to be *plausible*. If the most "likely-sounding" next words form a false claim, it says the false claim. Confident-sounding isn't the same as true. This is also why two similar questions can get different answers, and why it can change its mind if you push back.

For a fuller picture of how these models work, our [guide to what AI is](/articles/what-is-ai-explained) explains the word-by-word prediction in plain English.

## The situations where it bites people

**Fake citations and sources.** Ask a chatbot for academic references and it may generate a list of real-sounding papers — authors, journals, years — that never existed. If you're writing a research paper, this is a trap.

**Wrong code.** Chatbots are genuinely good at code, but they'll happily produce plausible-looking code with a subtle bug, or code that uses a function that doesn't exist. Code that "looks right" still needs to run.

**Health, legal, and financial advice.** A confidently wrong answer about a drug dose, a law, or an investment can cause real harm. Never rely on a chatbot for these without checking a qualified source.

**Recent or niche facts.** Models are trained on data up to a cutoff, and they're weakest on recent events and obscure topics — exactly where they're most likely to invent.

**Numbers and math.** AI can produce confident-looking math that's wrong, especially in multi-step calculations. It's better at seeming logical than being correct.

## How often does it happen?

It's hard to put a single number on how often models hallucinate — rates vary by model, topic, and how the question is asked. What's consistent is that *any* model can be confidently wrong, and the stakes of the answer should decide how hard you verify. Low-stakes brainstorming? Let it be wrong. Medical or financial advice? Verify everything.

## How to fact-check AI output: a practical workflow

1. **Treat it as a draft, not an answer.** Assume anything the AI states as fact could be wrong until you've checked it.
2. **Ask it to show its reasoning and sources.** Prompt with: "Where did you learn this? List sources." The sources may be fake, but the process often surfaces errors.
3. **Cross-check critical facts yourself.** For anything important, do your own search using a search engine, or a source-based tool that displays citations. Verify against the actual source, not just a summary of it.
4. **Verify any citation is real.** If it gives you a paper, a law, or a statistic, search for it directly. Be suspicious of citations you can't find.
5. **Run code before you trust it.** Test generated code in a safe environment — or at least review it line by line — before running it anywhere that matters.
6. **Ask the model to double-check itself.** "Are you sure? Check your math / check the dates." It won't always catch its own errors, but it catches some.
7. **Use it where being wrong is cheap.** Summaries, outlines, brainstorming, drafts — fine. Medicine, law, money, life decisions — never without verification.

## What to do when you catch a hallucination

First, don't panic — it's normal, not a malfunction. Tell the model it's wrong and give it the correct information; it'll often produce a better revision. Then note the topic for the future: models hallucinate most in the areas you just learned about. Finally, remember the pattern: the more confident and specific the claim, the more it's worth checking.

Also worth doing: keep a running mental list of topics where the model has been unreliable for you. Over time you'll learn which questions you can trust it on and which you should always double-check. That calibration is the real skill — it's how you use a sometimes-wrong tool without getting burned.

## Connecting the dots

Hallucinations are a big reason to be careful about the wider AI landscape. The same technology that makes up facts can also be used to [generate fake voices and deepfake calls](/articles/ai-scams-deepfake-calls) designed to scam you. Recognizing that AI output — text or voice — is not automatically true is one of the most important digital habits of 2026.

## Wrapping up

AI hallucinates because it predicts likely text instead of checking facts. That doesn't make it useless — it makes it a tool you have to verify.
