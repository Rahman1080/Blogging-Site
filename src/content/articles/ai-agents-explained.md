You've probably used a chatbot by now: type a question, get an answer. The next chapter of AI works differently. Instead of just *answering*, it *does* — it books the flight, fills out the form, compares the prices, and reports back when it's finished. These are **AI agents**, and they're the biggest shift in AI since ChatGPT.

Here's what agents actually are, what they can genuinely do today, and the things you should be careful about as they get more capable.

## What makes an agent different

A regular chatbot is a conversation: it takes your prompt, generates a response, and stops. An agent is a *worker*. It receives a goal, then works toward it in a loop:

1. **Plan** — break the goal into steps.
2. **Act** — use tools: browse the web, click buttons, type text, run software.
3. **Check** — look at the result and decide what to do next.
4. **Repeat** — until the task is done or it decides it's stuck.

The key ingredient is that agents can *interact with the world* — not just words. They can open websites, fill forms, move files, send emails, and use other apps, in the same way you would, but on their own.

## What agents can do today

Real, working agents already exist, and you can use several of them:

- **Research and comparison.** Tell an agent "find me a hotel in Lisbon under $150 a night with good reviews," and it can browse booking sites, compile options, and summarize — with links you can check.
- **Computer use.** Anthropic's Claude can be given control of a computer screen to click through software and complete tasks, like a virtual assistant watching your monitor. OpenAI's Operator does similar work in a dedicated browser.
- **Filling forms and admin.** Agents can draft and submit repetitive paperwork, transfer information between apps, and handle scheduling.
- **Coding assistance.** Developer tools have shipped agent modes that plan and write larger chunks of code than a chatbot ever could.
- **Travel planning.** From itinerary to booking, agents can string together the research and transactions, subject to your approval.

None of these are demos — they're shipping features in products you can install today. But the reality is more modest than the marketing: they're useful on clearly defined tasks and still error-prone on messy, multi-step ones.

## What they can't do yet

The honest version, because agents are hyped hard:

- **They make mistakes.** An agent can misinterpret a request, click the wrong button, or confidently produce the wrong result. A human still needs to review important outcomes.
- **They're slow and can be expensive.** Multi-step tasks take minutes, and heavy agent usage can rack up API costs on the platforms that charge for it.
- **They don't understand everything they touch.** An agent can read a terms-of-service page and agree to it without understanding it.
- **They're only as good as their tools.** Agent performance depends on what websites and apps they're allowed to use — and a broken or hostile page can derail them.

## What's coming in the next few years

This is where it gets interesting, and it's why experts consider agents the next wave rather than a passing feature:

**On-device agents.** Apple, Google, and Microsoft are building agents into phones and operating systems — assistants that can act across your apps with your permission, informed by your data on the device rather than in the cloud. This is where the "agent for everyone" story becomes practical.

**More autonomy with guardrails.** Expect agents to handle longer chains of tasks with clearer safeguards: spending limits, permission prompts for sensitive actions, and better ability to explain what they're doing.

**Agent-to-agent work.** The next phase is agents that coordinate with other agents — your scheduling agent coordinating with a company's customer-service agent. That's still early, but it's the direction.

## What to watch out for

The same features that make agents powerful make them risky:

- **Permissions are everything.** Only give an agent access to what the task needs, and revoke it when done.
- **Don't let them spend unsupervised.** Use the built-in limits where they exist, and require your confirmation on any transaction.
- **Phishing works on agents too.** A malicious website can try to trick an agent into bad actions. Agents with web access need their own safeguards.
- **Privacy matters.** An agent that can read your email or files is powerful — and a risk if compromised. Prefer on-device options for sensitive data.

## How to try one

Start small and reversible. Use a research or travel-planning agent on a low-stakes task, review every step it takes, and see where it gets things right and wrong. You'll quickly develop a feel for what to delegate and what to keep for yourself.

## Wrapping up

AI agents are the difference between software that advises you and software that works for you. They're genuinely useful today for bounded tasks, and they'll become far more capable over the next few years. The trick is treating them like a capable but careless assistant: useful, worth watching, and never trusted blindly.
