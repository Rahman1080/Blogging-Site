# How to Secure Your Home Wi-Fi Network

Your Wi-Fi network is the front door to every device in your home — your laptop, your phone, your smart doorbell, your bank apps. If that door is left open, the consequences range from neighbors stealing your bandwidth to someone quietly poking at your connected devices.

The good news: securing it is a 15-minute, one-time job. Here's the checklist, in the order that matters.

## Why it matters

A home network is only as strong as its weakest entry point. Most people secure their computers and phones but leave the network itself wide open — a weak password, an old security standard, or WPS turned on.

An insecure network doesn't just slow you down. An attacker on your Wi-Fi can see devices on the network, intercept traffic on older encryption, and use your connection for anything — including illegal activity that traces back to your address. None of that is likely, but all of it is cheaply preventable.

## 1. Set a strong, unique Wi-Fi password

The foundation of everything else. If your Wi-Fi password is short, guessable, or shared with everyone who ever visited, start here.

1. Log into your router's admin page (the address and login are on the sticker on the router).
2. Find the Wireless or Wi-Fi settings.
3. Set the password (pre-shared key) to a long passphrase — a dozen or more characters, with a few words and numbers mixed in. Use a new one, not the same password you use for accounts.
4. Save. Connected devices will drop and need the new password once.

A strong network password means strangers can't join your network at all, which removes most home-network problems before they start.

## 2. Use WPA2 or WPA3 encryption

Your password is only as good as the encryption that scrambles it. In the same wireless settings, check the security mode:

- **WPA3** is the newest standard and the most secure. Use it if your router offers it.
- **WPA2** is the previous standard and still fine, especially if some of your devices are older.
- **WPA2/WPA3 mixed** mode lets new devices use WPA3 while old ones connect with WPA2 — a good middle ground.
- **WEP** and **WPA** are old and easily broken. If your router only offers these, your router is very old — consider replacing it.

If you have a mix of devices and some are years old, mixed mode avoids the classic problem where an older device can't connect to a strict WPA3-only network.

## 3. Turn off WPS

WPS (Wi-Fi Protected Setup) was designed to make connecting printers and other gadgets easy — often by entering a short PIN. That convenience comes with a serious flaw: the PIN can be guessed, giving attackers a way onto your network without your password.

1. In the router settings, look for **WPS** or **Wi-Fi Protected Setup**.
2. Turn it off.
3. If you later need to connect a device, use the push-button method or just enter the Wi-Fi password.

There's essentially no reason to leave WPS on for a typical home network.

## 4. Rename your network — and don't advertise your identity

The network name (SSID) is what neighbors see in their Wi-Fi list. Two practical tips:

- **Change it from the default.** Default names like "NETGEAR-1234" or "Home-2.4" tell people which router you have, which helps them look up default passwords and known flaws.
- **Don't put your name or address in it.** A name like "The Smith Family, 12 Oak Street" is a billboard for anyone nearby. Something neutral — a nickname, a house number, a random word — is better.

You don't need to hide the network entirely (that causes more connection problems than it prevents), but a neutral name is a small, free improvement.

## 5. Update the router firmware

Router manufacturers release firmware updates with security patches and bug fixes. An unpatched router is one of the most common entry points into a home network, because routers often run for years without an update.

1. Log into the admin page.
2. Look for **Firmware Update** or **Administration → Update**.
3. Install any available update and let the router restart.

Some routers update automatically; check whether yours does. If not, make this a once-a-year habit, or set a reminder to check.

## 6. Set up a guest network

Most routers can broadcast a second network for visitors — a "guest network" that has internet access but no access to your main devices.

- Guests (and their devices) get online without touching your network password.
- Anything on the guest network is walled off from your computers, phones, and smart-home devices.
- If a visitor's phone is compromised or suspicious, it can't reach the rest of your network.

Enable it in the router settings, give it its own password (a simple one is fine — this is for convenience), and you've separated your main network from everything that enters your home for an afternoon.

## 7. Check who's connected

A quick periodic look shows whether anyone you don't recognize is on your network:

1. Log into the router's admin page.
2. Open **Connected Devices**, **Device List**, or **Attached Devices**.
3. Review the names. Phones show as "iPhone" or "Pixel," computers by brand or model. Unknown names could be a forgotten guest — or a freeloader — and most routers let you block or remove them directly.

Do this after a house party, a long visit, or any time you shared the password. Our [guide to router setup](/articles/how-to-set-up-a-new-router) walks through the admin page if you're not sure where to find these menus.

## The password part of the picture

Securing the network covers the walls of the house, but your accounts still need their own locks. The same weak-password habit that undermines a network password also undermines your online accounts — which is exactly why a [password manager](/articles/password-manager-guide) is still the single best upgrade you can make. And if anyone ever calls or emails you claiming to be from "tech support" about a problem with your network, that's not how it works — [phishing calls and messages](/articles/how-to-spot-phishing-email) are exactly how people get tricked into handing over access.

## Wrapping up

Fifteen minutes, in order: strong network password, WPA2 or WPA3, WPS off, a neutral network name, updated firmware, a guest network for visitors, and an occasional check of connected devices. Do those and your home network is securely ahead of the vast majority of households.
