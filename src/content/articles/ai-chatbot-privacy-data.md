Before you paste a private document, a work contract, or a medical note into a chatbot, you should know what happens to it after you press enter. The honest answer: it depends on which tool you use and what settings you've chosen — but the default in consumer chatbots is that your conversations can be stored, reviewed, and used to improve the service.

This is the plain-language version of what that means, what controls exist, and what's genuinely safe to paste.

## What chatbots typically collect

Different providers handle things differently, but consumer AI chatbots generally collect:

- **Your conversations.** The prompts you type and the responses you get.
- **Account and usage data.** When you use the tool, what device, roughly where you are, and how long you spend.
- **Related context.** Some tools pull in data from connected apps, uploaded files, or linked accounts if you enable them.
- **Feedback.** When you upvote or downvote a response, that's used to train and improve.

The big, consistent point: in most free consumer tiers, what you type is treated as data the company can use — including for training — unless you change a setting. Specific policies change often and differ between products, so the reliable habit is to check the privacy settings in the tool you use rather than trust any list of rules.

## Is it "safe" to put personal info in ChatGPT?

Safer than posting it publicly, but not private. Think of a chat with an AI the way you'd think of a conversation with a messaging service you don't fully trust: it's a company's server, subject to its policies, its reviewers, and its security. The question isn't "is the provider a villain" — it's "do I want this particular piece of information stored by a third party?"

Good rule of thumb: **never paste anything you wouldn't put in an unencrypted email.**

## What you should never paste

- Passwords, security codes, or PINs
- ID numbers (passport, driver's license, Social Security, tax IDs)
- Financial details: bank accounts, credit card numbers, full salaries
- Health information or medical records
- Trade secrets, unreleased product plans, or confidential work documents
- Personal data about other people without their knowledge — and in many cases, their legal consent

If your company uses a consumer chatbot for work, this matters doubly — many employers explicitly forbid feeding internal data into them. ## The settings that actually matter

Every major chatbot provider offers privacy-related settings. They're worth finding once, even if you never change them:

- **History / chat storage off.** Usually under Settings → Privacy or Data Controls. Turning this off tells the provider not to save your conversations for training (in some products it also disables memory features). Note: it often limits some functionality, and it doesn't necessarily erase data already collected.
- **Delete conversations.** Most tools let you delete individual chats or all history. It's not a guarantee of deletion everywhere, but it's the available lever.
- **Export your data.** Many providers let you download a copy of your conversation history — worth knowing if you ever want to leave.
- **Training opt-outs.** Where they exist, these tell the provider not to use your data to improve the model.
- **Enterprise / work versions.** If your company offers a business plan (like ChatGPT Team/Enterprise or the equivalent from other vendors), these typically promise your data is not used for training and offer stronger controls. If you use AI for work, ask your IT team which plan you're on.

## How to check your provider's settings

1. Open the chatbot app or website and go to **Settings** (often under your profile picture or the menu).
2. Look for a **Privacy**, **Data Controls**, or **Data & Privacy** section. The names vary, so search the tool's help center if you can't find it.
3. Read what each toggle actually says — providers phrase these differently.
4. Set the ones you care about, then test that the behavior changed (for example, that history no longer appears after you close a chat).

## What about voice and image input?

The same rules apply. Voice conversations and uploaded photos are input data, stored the same way text is. A voice memo of a sensitive meeting or a photo of a confidential document is data you've handed over, full stop.

## A note on scams and phishing

Data privacy is also why you should be suspicious of anything that claims to be an "AI chatbot" but wants unusual permissions or asks you to install something. Scammers increasingly use AI to sound convincing — see our guide to [AI scam calls and deepfakes](/articles/ai-scams-deepfake-calls) for how to spot a fake. Treat any app that asks for more data than a conversation needs as a red flag.

## Wrapping up

Consumer chatbots store and can use what you type. That's not a reason to stop using them — it's a reason to be deliberate: check the privacy settings, keep secrets out of the chat window, and use a work account if you're handling company data. The habit of treating AI conversations like an unencrypted email will serve you better than any single policy.
