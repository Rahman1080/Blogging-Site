App updates are a bit like dental checkups: nobody gets excited about them, but skipping them ends badly. Auto-update on Android exists to make sure apps patch themselves without you remembering to do it — and it takes about thirty seconds to set up the way you want.

Here's how to turn it on, how to stop it, and the honest trade-offs either way.

## How to turn on auto-update

The setting lives in the Google Play Store app — not in your phone's Settings.

1. Open the **Google Play Store** app.
2. Tap your **profile icon** in the top right corner.
3. Tap **Settings**.
4. Tap **Network preferences**.
5. Tap **Auto-update apps**.
6. Choose one of:
   - **Over any network** — updates run over Wi-Fi and mobile data.
   - **Over Wi-Fi only** — updates wait until the phone is on Wi-Fi.
   - **Don't auto-update apps** — updates only happen when you run them manually.

Pick **Over Wi-Fi only** unless you have unlimited mobile data — it's the sweet spot for most people. Your apps still update themselves regularly, but they never surprise you by burning your data plan.

Once it's on, apps update themselves in the background, usually overnight while the phone is charging and on Wi-Fi. You don't have to do anything.

## Why you should keep updates on (usually)

Auto-update isn't about convenience alone — it's a security habit.

- **Security patches.** Most app updates exist because the developer found and fixed a security problem. Every update you skip is a known vulnerability left open on your phone. Scammers and malware increasingly exploit outdated apps, and updates are the patch for that.
- **Bug fixes.** The crashes, freezes, and glitches you hit are often already fixed — you just need the update.
- **Compatibility.** New Android versions change how apps run. Apps that fall behind start misbehaving on newer phones.
- **Features.** Updates also bring the new stuff — useful, but the reason above is the one that matters.

The "set it and forget it" model is genuinely the safest default, which is why most phones ship with auto-update already on.

## The honest trade-offs

Auto-update isn't perfect, and it's worth knowing what you're trading for the convenience:

- **Surprise changes.** Apps occasionally redesign their interface or move features, and you wake up to a layout you didn't ask for. You usually can't "un-update" easily, so if you liked the old version, that's gone.
- **Data use.** Updates over mobile data add up — though that's exactly what the "Over Wi-Fi only" option solves.
- **A bad update.** Rarely, an app update ships broken and crashes until the developer pushes a fix. When that happens, auto-update means you got the bad version automatically. The fixes for that are the same as for any crashing app.

For most people the security benefit outweighs all of it. If you'd rather keep control, the manual route below is a perfectly reasonable choice too — just stay disciplined about updating regularly.

## How to update manually (when you prefer control)

Prefer to decide what updates and when? That's a valid choice. Here's the manual flow:

1. Open the **Google Play Store** app.
2. Tap your **profile icon** → **Manage apps & device**.
3. If updates are available, you'll see an **Update all** button — or update individual apps from the "Updates available" list.
4. To avoid surprise updates entirely, go back to **Settings → Network preferences → Auto-update apps** and choose **Don't auto-update apps**.

You can also update from an individual app's Play Store page — open the app in the store and tap **Update** if it's available. Some phones let you disable auto-update for a single app from its Play Store page (tap the three-dot menu on the app's page and look for the auto-update option), which is the best of both worlds if your version supports it.

## "Why are my apps updating without my permission?"

This is a common question, and the answer is almost always simple: auto-update was already on. It's the default on most phones, and some apps re-enable related settings during their own setup. If apps update while you sleep, check **Settings → Network preferences** to see what's enabled — and if it was you who turned it on earlier, that's your answer.

One more thing that can look like "updating without permission": an app that asks you to accept new permissions during an update. The Play Store shows this on the app's page before the update installs, which is why some updates need a tap even with auto-update on. You can review and accept — or deny, which also blocks that update.

## If an update breaks an app

An app that starts misbehaving right after an update usually needs a quick cache clear rather than a rollback (rolling back to an older version is awkward and often unsupported).

- Try clearing the app's cache first — it fixes a surprising share of post-update glitches. Our [guide to clearing cache on Android](/articles/how-to-clear-cache-android) shows how in a minute.
- If that doesn't help, the next update from the developer usually fixes it — and yes, that means letting auto-update do its job again.

Keeping apps updated is also one of the quieter ways to keep a phone running smoothly, which is why it shows up in [our guide to fixing a slow Android phone](/articles/android-phone-running-slow). More app and phone help is in the [Android category](/articles/category/android).

## Wrapping up

Open the Play Store, tap your profile, and set auto-update to **Over Wi-Fi only** — that's the setting most people want. Apps patch themselves securely overnight, they never burn your mobile data, and you never have to think about it again.

If you prefer manual control, that's fine too — just make updating a habit, not a chore. Either way, the apps on your phone will thank you.
