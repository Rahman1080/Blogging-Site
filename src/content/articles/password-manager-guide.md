If you're like most people, you reuse a handful of passwords across every site you've ever joined. It's convenient — and it's the single biggest reason accounts get hacked. This guide explains why password managers are the fix, and walks you through setting one up.

## Why your current system is a ticking bomb

When you reuse the same password on a shopping site and your email, a breach of the shopping site gives attackers the key to your email. From your email they can reset passwords on *everything else*. This is called **credential stuffing**, and it's automated: attackers try stolen email/password combinations across hundreds of sites in seconds.

The only reliable defense is **a different, strong password for every account**. That's impossible to memorize — which is exactly where password managers come in.

## What a password manager does

A password manager is a secure vault that:

- Stores all your usernames and passwords in one encrypted database.
- Generates strong, unique passwords for each site.
- Auto-fills logins so you don't type passwords manually.
- Syncs your vault across phone, tablet, and computer.
- Alerts you to breaches and weak or reused passwords.

You remember **one master password**. The manager remembers everything else.

## Why it's safe (in plain terms)

Your vault is encrypted with your master password using strong encryption (AES-256). The key insight: **the manager's servers never see your master password** — a design called "zero-knowledge." Even if the company's servers are breached, attackers only get scrambled data they can't read.

Losing your phone doesn't mean losing your passwords; you can sign in on a new device with your master password (plus a recovery key or backup code).

## Choosing a manager

The top names are all solid: **1Password, Bitwarden, Dashlane**, and **KeePass** (open source). For most people:

- **Bitwarden** — free tier is genuinely excellent, open source.
- **1Password** — polished, great for families, paid.
- **KeePass** — fully offline, if you want maximum control (more manual).

Pick one. The best password manager is the one you'll actually use.

## Setting one up in 10 minutes

Here's the process, using Bitwarden as an example (others are similar):

### 1. Create your account

1. Go to the manager's website and sign up.
2. Create a **master password** — this is the only password you need to remember.
3. Follow the setup wizard to save a **recovery key / emergency kit**. Store it somewhere safe (a printed note in a drawer is fine — that's the point).

### 2. Install the browser extension and apps

- Add the extension to your browser (Chrome, Edge, Firefox).
- Install the phone app on iOS and Android.
- Sign in on each with your master password.

### 3. Save your first login

1. Open the extension and create a new vault entry for your email account.
2. Let it **generate a strong random password** (16+ characters, mixed).
3. Save it, then log into that account and update its password to the generated one.

### 4. Audit your current passwords

Most managers have a **vault health / security report**:

- Find accounts using **reused passwords** and fix the important ones first (email, banking, social media).
- Find accounts in known **breaches** and change those immediately.

You don't have to fix everything in one sitting — prioritize the accounts that matter most.

### 5. Enable two-factor authentication

A password manager pairs perfectly with **2FA**. Turn it on for your manager account itself first, then for your email and banking. This means even a stolen master password isn't enough.

## Making it a habit

- Let the manager **generate passwords** for every new account from day one.
- Use the **auto-fill** — it's faster than typing, once you're used to it.
- Revisit the security report once a month.
