Your photos are the files you'd least want to lose, so getting them off the phone onto a computer or into iCloud is worth doing properly. Here's every reliable method, the honest differences between them, and the one gotcha everyone hits — HEIC files.

You can do this three ways: download from iCloud.com (no cable, no apps), plug in a USB cable (works on Mac and Windows), or AirDrop (Mac only). None of them remove photos from your iPhone, and none of them are hard.

## What you'll need

- Your iPhone and its charging cable.
- A Mac, or a Windows PC.
- An Apple ID and password if you're using iCloud.com.

## Method 1: Download from iCloud.com (easiest, no cable)

This is the most straightforward route because it doesn't care what computer you're on. It does assume your photos are already in iCloud — which they are if iCloud Photos is switched on.

1. Open a browser on your computer and go to **iCloud.com**.
2. Sign in with your Apple ID and turn on any verification prompt on your phone.
3. Open **Photos**.
4. **Select** the photos you want. Click the first one, hold Shift and click the last one for a range, or hold Ctrl (Command on Mac) and click individual photos.
5. Click the **download** icon (a cloud with an arrow pointing down) at the top.
6. The photos arrive in your browser's Downloads folder as a ZIP file if you selected several. Unzip it.

To get everything, you'd select all and download in batches — iCloud.com doesn't offer a one-click "download my whole library." If you have thousands of photos, the USB cable method below is usually faster.

If iCloud Photos is off on your phone, iCloud.com will only show the photos you've manually uploaded there. Check **Settings → Photos** on the iPhone first.

## Method 2: USB cable (works on Mac and Windows)

No cloud, no Wi-Fi, no messing about — just a wire and your computer's built-in tools.

### On a Mac

1. Connect the iPhone to the Mac with the cable and tap **Trust** on the phone if prompted.
2. Open the **Photos** app on the Mac.
3. Your iPhone appears in the sidebar under Devices. Select it.
4. You'll see the new photos. Click **Import All**, or select specific ones first. Choose whether to **Delete after import** (removing them from the phone) or keep copies on both.
5. The imported photos land in your Photos library.

Prefer plain files? Open **Image Capture** (it's in your Applications folder or Launchpad) instead — it lets you drag photos straight out of the phone into any folder on the Mac.

### On a Windows PC

1. Connect the iPhone with the cable and tap **Trust** on the phone.
2. If AutoPlay pops up, choose **Import photos and videos**. If it doesn't, open the **Photos** app on Windows and click the **Import** button, then select the phone.
3. Pick the photos you want and click **Import**.
4. The photos land in the Pictures folder (or wherever you told Windows to put them).

Both Windows methods work without iTunes — the PC treats the iPhone as a camera, just like a digital camera. The only thing you might need is the HEIC-to-JPEG extension, which Windows will prompt you to install if you open a photo it can't display.

## Method 3: AirDrop to a Mac (wireless, no cable)

AirDrop is the fastest way to move a handful of photos — or a few hundred — over to a Mac. Both devices need to be reasonably close.

1. On the **Mac**, open **Finder** and click **AirDrop** in the sidebar. Make sure it's set to receive from Contacts Only or Everyone.
2. On the **iPhone**, open the **Photos** app, tap **Select**, and pick the photos.
3. Tap the **Share** button (the square with an arrow up), then tap **AirDrop**.
4. Tap your Mac's name. Accept the transfer on the Mac if asked.
5. The photos land in the **Downloads** folder by default.

AirDrop keeps the original files and doesn't compress them. If it's your Mac and your phone, they usually see each other instantly.

## The HEIC vs JPEG thing, explained honestly

Here's the one part that trips everyone up. Modern iPhones save photos as **HEIC** by default — a format that's about half the file size of a JPEG with the same quality. It's great for your storage and your iCloud bill.

The catch: **Windows and some older programs can't open HEIC files** without extra support. You'll see files like `IMG_1234.HEIC` and double-clicking gets you a blank or a "can't open" message.

You have two good options:

- **Let the iPhone convert:** In **Settings → Photos**, find **Transfer to Mac or PC** and set it to **Automatic**. The phone then converts to JPEG as it transfers over USB — slightly slower, but everything just opens.
- **Keep originals and add support:** Keep HEIC and install the HEIF extension on Windows (it's in the Microsoft Store and Windows will point you to it), or open them in a modern app like Adobe Photoshop or the latest Google Photos.

If you transfer via **iCloud.com**, the download keeps the original format. Use the "Transfer to Mac or PC" setting in Settings → Photos before a cable transfer to get JPEGs automatically.

## Which method should you use?

A quick rule of thumb:

- **A handful of photos, Mac user** → AirDrop.
- **Your whole library, no cloud** → USB cable, either platform.
- **No cable handy, or any computer** → iCloud.com.
- **Setting up a new machine** → iCloud sync (turn on iCloud Photos) does it all for you.

## Wrapping up

Whatever you choose, you end up with copies of your photos somewhere safer than your phone.
