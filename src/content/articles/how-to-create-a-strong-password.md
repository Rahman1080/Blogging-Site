# How to Create a Strong Password (That You Can Actually Remember)

The advice "use a strong password" is easy to give and miserable to follow, because the passwords everyone told you were strong — `P@ssw0rd1`, `Tr0ub4dor&3` — are both hard to remember and not nearly as strong as they look. The fix isn't to invent cleverer passwords. It's to stop treating passwords like codes and start treating them like sentences.

The short version: pick three or four random words, join them with dots or hyphens, and you'll have a password that's easy to remember and genuinely hard to crack. Here's why that works and how to do it without a spreadsheet.

## What actually makes a password strong

Before we talk formulas, it helps to know how passwords get broken. Two main things happen:

- **Guessing.** An attacker tries common passwords, dictionary words, and personal details like your name or birthday. Short and predictable passwords fail here.
- **Cracking.** When a website's password data leaks, attackers run automated tools that test millions of combinations per second. Short passwords fall in minutes, no matter how many symbols they contain.

Both attacks share the same weakness: they get dramatically harder as your password gets *longer*. A seven-character password with numbers and symbols has fewer possible combinations than a long string of ordinary lowercase words. Length beats complexity almost every time.

## The passphrase formula

A passphrase is just a password built from several words. The formula is simple:

**Three or four random words, joined with a separator.**

### 1. Pick three or four random words

Choose words that are unrelated — not "blue sky sunshine" but "lamp bracket jellyfish piano." Avoid famous phrases, song lyrics, and anything tied to your life like pet names or street names. Randomness is the whole point.

### 2. Join them with a separator

Dots, hyphens, or underscores all work: `lamp-bracket-jellyfish-piano`. The separator mostly matters because it makes the phrase easier to read while you type it.

### 3. Add one thing you'll actually remember (optional)

You can improve a passphrase slightly with a single twist you won't forget — making one word's first letter uppercase, or tagging a number on the end. Don't get complicated. If the twist makes you pause every time you log in, skip it.

## Strong vs. weak password examples

It's easier to feel the difference with real examples.

**Weak:**

- `password`
- `password123`
- `John2019!`
- `qwerty123`

**Strong:**

- `lamp-bracket-jellyfish-piano`
- `River!toast!magnet!gerbil`
- `Bicycle-cloud-pancake-orchard`

Notice the strong ones are all things you could say aloud in a sentence. That's exactly what makes them memorable. Notice none of them depend on a number or symbol that a person — or a cracking tool — could guess from knowing you.

One more honest point about symbols and numbers: they still help, just far less than length does. If you're comfortable adding one, add it — but don't sacrifice length to satisfy the old "one number, one symbol, one capital" checklist. A 20-character all-lowercase passphrase is genuinely harder to crack than a 12-character one that hits every complexity rule, and it's far more pleasant to type on a phone. The two examples that begin with a capital letter above show the style: it reads naturally, which means you'll remember it.

## How to remember your passwords

The whole point of a passphrase is that you don't need to write it on a sticky note or reset it every month. A few habits make it stick:

- **Use a passphrase for the accounts you type by hand** — your computer login, your phone, maybe your email.
- **Type it a few times.** Muscle memory handles the rest. After a week it'll feel automatic.
- **Don't rotate it constantly.** Changing a strong password for no reason just pushes people into weaker habits. Change it when there's a real reason, like a data breach or a shared device.
- **Never reuse it.** One password per account. If a password appears in a breach on one site, you don't want the same one protecting your email and your bank.

Reusing passwords is the single most dangerous habit in online security, because it means one leak can unlock everything. If you currently have one go-to password for everything, this article's real job is convincing you to stop.

## The bigger upgrade: a password manager

Let's be honest: passphrases are great, but you can't remember fifty different ones. That's not a character flaw, it's a limit everyone hits.

The realistic solution is a [password manager](/articles/password-manager-guide) — a tool that stores strong, unique, randomly generated passwords for every site and unlocks them with a single master passphrase. You remember one phrase; the manager remembers the other thousand. It also autofills your logins, which means you're less likely to fall for a fake login page — a separate reason managers make you safer.

Passphrases and password managers aren't competing ideas. Use a passphrase as your master password, and let the manager generate everything else.

## The end of passwords?

You may have heard that passwords are on the way out. It's partly true — [passkeys](/articles/passkeys-explained) let you log in with your phone's fingerprint or face instead of a password, and they're appearing on more sites all the time. Where a site offers a passkey, use it; it's both easier and safer.

But most of the internet still runs on passwords, and even with passkeys you'll likely keep one or two as a backup or recovery option. Building better passwords is still a skill worth having.

## Wrapping up

Strong doesn't mean complicated. Pick three or four random words, join them with a separator, and you'll have a password that's easy to remember and hard to crack. Use it for your master passphrase, never reuse passwords across sites, and let a password manager handle the rest.
