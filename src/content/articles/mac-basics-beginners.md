So you've got your first Mac. Whether you switched from Windows or you've simply never used macOS before, the good news is that the basics fit on one page. You don't need to learn a hundred things today — you need a few things done right, and the rest falls into place.

Here's the honest overview: macOS looks different from Windows, but the logic underneath is the same. There's a file system, apps, and settings — they just live in different places. Work through the sections below and you'll feel at home within a week.

## The menu bar and the Apple menu

The thin bar along the very top of your screen is the **menu bar**. This is the single biggest difference from Windows, and it's the key to understanding a Mac.

Unlike Windows, where each app has its own menu inside its window, the Mac's menu bar is global — it always sits at the top of the screen, and its contents change to match whichever app is currently active. If a menu option you're looking for seems missing, click on the app's window first; the menu bar updates to show that app's menus.

On the far left of the menu bar is the **Apple menu** (the  symbol). Click it any time for system-wide options: About This Mac, System Settings, Sleep, Restart, and Shut Down. It's always available no matter what app is open, which makes it a handy anchor when you're lost.

On the right side of the menu bar you'll find the status items — Wi-Fi, battery, volume, date and time, and any icons from apps like your calendar or cloud storage. These are macOS's version of the Windows system tray.

## The Dock

The row of icons along the bottom of the screen is the **Dock** — your launchpad for apps you use often. Click any icon to open that app. A small dot under an icon means the app is currently running.

A few things to know:

- **Your most-used apps** — Finder, your browser, Mail, and so on — sit there by default.
- **To add an app**: open it from Launchpad or Applications, then drag its icon into the Dock.
- **To remove an app**: drag its icon off the Dock and let go. This only removes the shortcut — it does *not* uninstall the app.
- **Right-click (or two-finger click) a Dock icon** to see options like Quit and Options → Keep in Dock.

The first icon in the Dock is **Finder**, your file manager. It's always running, which is normal. If you ever want to browse your files, that's where you start.

## Trackpad gestures

Modern Mac trackpads are built around gestures, and they're worth learning because they're genuinely faster once you're used to them.

- **Left click**: a single tap, or a firm press.
- **Right click**: tap with two fingers.
- **Scroll**: swipe with two fingers.
- **Mission Control** (all open windows): swipe up with three fingers, or press the Mission Control key (F3).
- **Switch apps**: swipe left or right with three fingers to move between full-screen apps and spaces.
- **Pinch with two fingers** in a document to zoom in and out.
- **Tap with two fingers on a word** — or force-click — to look up a definition.

If a gesture doesn't do what you expect, check **System Settings → Trackpad**. You can adjust the clicking pressure, turning any gesture on or off, and switching the scroll direction (a favorite setting for Windows switchers).

## Installing apps

There are two main ways to install apps on a Mac, and the difference trips up a lot of new users.

**From the App Store.** Open the App Store, search, and click Get. The app installs automatically and appears in your Applications folder and Launchpad. Clean and simple — most free apps work this way.

**From the web.** Download a file, usually ending in `.dmg`, from the app's website. Double-click the disk image to mount it, then drag the app icon into your **Applications** folder. That drag is the actual install step.

Here's the common gotcha: if you just open the app straight from the downloaded disk image without dragging it into Applications, it still runs — but it's living in your Downloads, and when you eventually delete that disk image, the app disappears too. Dragging it into Applications is what makes it a permanent part of your Mac.

The first time you open an app downloaded from the internet, macOS may warn you that it's from an unidentified developer. If you trust the source, you can open it via System Settings → Privacy & Security. If you don't trust it, don't force it.

## Quitting apps for real

New Mac users get confused here constantly: you click the red button in the corner of a window and expect the app to close. Instead, the window closes, but the app keeps running in the Dock, dot and all.

That's not a bug. It's how macOS works — closing a window and quitting an app are separate actions.

- **Close window**: red button, or Cmd+W.
- **Quit the app**: Cmd+Q, or right-click the Dock icon and choose Quit.

A few apps (like your browser) are designed to keep running in the background; others, like your email app, check for new mail faster if you leave them open. There's no rule that says you must quit everything. But when you want an app truly gone — say, to free memory — use Cmd+Q.

## Getting around the file system

Finder is your home base. In a Finder window you'll see **Favorites** on the sidebar (AirDrop, Recents, Applications, Desktop, Documents, Downloads). Your Mac's user account lives in a folder with your name — that's where Documents, Desktop, and Downloads live.

The **Go menu** at the top of a Finder window gives you quick jumps to Home, Applications, and Utilities. And **Cmd+Space** opens Spotlight, which searches your whole Mac — apps, files, and even web results. For most people, Spotlight is the fastest way to open any app: press Cmd+Space, start typing the name, press Return.

## Tips for Windows switchers

If you're coming from Windows, a few translations will save you constant Googling:

- **Ctrl becomes Cmd.** Copy, paste, save, select all — every keyboard shortcut uses Cmd instead of Ctrl.
- **Close and minimize are separate.** The red, yellow, and green buttons close, minimize, and enter full screen — they don't quit the app.
- **There's no universal "Start" menu.** The Apple menu, Spotlight (Cmd+Space), and Launchpad cover that role.
- **Uninstalling is a drag.** To remove an app you installed yourself, drag it from Applications to the Trash (then empty the Trash). App Store apps can be uninstalled the same way or from Launchpad.

Your first few days will feel slower. Give it time — most people adjust within a week or two.

## Wrapping up

The Mac basics come down to five things: the menu bar at the top, the Dock at the bottom, trackpad gestures, installing apps by dragging them into Applications, and quitting them with Cmd+Q. Master those and you've mastered 90% of daily macOS use.
