# How to Choose an Internet Plan: How Much Speed Do You Actually Need?

The honest answer to "how much speed do I need?" is: a lot less than the sales page wants you to think. Most households genuinely use a fraction of what's advertised, and the plan number is rarely the reason your internet feels slow.

Here's a practical way to match a plan to your real life — by household size and what you actually do — without falling for the "biggest is best" trap.

## The uncomfortable truth about speed numbers

Internet plans are advertised by their top speed in Mbps (megabits per second). That number is a maximum, not a promise. It's what the connection can do in ideal conditions, with one device, one foot from the router, at a quiet hour.

Your real-life experience is shaped by Wi-Fi distance, walls, how many devices are sharing the network, and how busy your street is. A 300 Mbps plan can feel like 30 Mbps at the far end of the house on a crowded evening. That's not the plan's fault — but it's also why upgrading your plan often doesn't fix "slow internet." Fix the Wi-Fi first, then decide if the plan is genuinely too small. Our [home internet speed guide](/articles/how-to-speed-up-home-internet) shows how to separate the two.

## Upload vs download (and why uploads matter now)

Download speed is how fast you receive data — streaming, loading pages, getting files. Upload speed is how fast you send it — video calls, cloud backups, sending photos and documents.

Most plans advertise download speed and quietly pair it with a much smaller upload number. That used to be fine. But now that video calls, cloud storage, and working from home are normal, uploads matter more than the average bill lets on. A choppy video call is usually an upload problem.

When comparing plans, check both numbers. Fiber plans tend to offer generous uploads; cable plans often skimp on them. If your life involves video calls and cloud backups, a plan with a decent upload number is worth a small premium.

## A rough idea by household

These are ranges, not laws — think of them as starting points.

- **One or two people, light use:** streaming, browsing, email, social media. A modest plan handles this easily. The low end of most providers' lineups is usually plenty.
- **One or two people, heavy use:** 4K streaming, gaming, regular video calls. A step up from the entry tier gives comfortable headroom.
- **Family of three to five:** several streams at once, gaming, homework, remote work. A mid-tier plan in the 100–300 Mbps range covers the large majority of families comfortably.
- **Busy house, heavy everything:** multiple 4K streams plus competitive gaming plus daily video calls plus big uploads. The top tiers are for this scenario — and even then, only a handful of homes truly need them.

The pattern to notice: the jump from "plenty" to "way more than you'll use" happens fast. A mid-range plan is the sweet spot for most people.

## Match the plan to what you do

### Streaming

Video streaming uses far less than most people assume. A single HD stream is modest; even 4K streams on a typical TV sit within what a mid-tier plan handles. The number that matters more is how many streams run at once in your house. Two to four simultaneous streams is routine, not extreme — and mid-tier plans absorb it.

### Gaming

Gaming itself uses very little bandwidth — the game is mostly downloading once, then exchanging small amounts of data. What matters is latency (lag), which a bigger plan doesn't fix. A stable connection, low-latency game server, and steady Wi-Fi or a cable beat a faster plan every time. If you lag while others stream, QoS and a wired connection help more than a speed upgrade.

### Video calls and working from home

Video calls are sensitive to upload speed and stability, not just download. If you're on calls daily while others stream, make sure the plan's upload number is reasonable and consider prioritizing your work device on the router. Working from home is the scenario where the top-end plans occasionally justify themselves — but a mid-tier plan with good uploads handles most jobs.

### The busy family

The real test isn't any single activity; it's everything happening at once. Five devices streaming, gaming, and calling simultaneously is what actually stresses a plan. If that's your evening and it struggles, the two options are a faster plan or, more often, better use of the one you have.

## Why the plan number isn't the speed you get

Before you pay for more speed, run this test once:

1. Plug a computer directly into the router with an Ethernet cable.
2. Run a speed test.
3. If the wired result is close to your plan, your connection is fine — the slowness is Wi-Fi, and more speed won't fix it.
4. If the wired result is far below your plan, then talk to your provider.

This ten-minute test decides whether your problem is a plan issue or a Wi-Fi issue, and it stops most people from overpaying for speed they'll never reach over the air.

Also worth knowing: the connection type changes the picture. A fiber plan behaves differently from cable at peak hours, and what's available at your address limits your options entirely. Our [fiber vs cable comparison](/articles/fiber-vs-cable-internet) explains which technology you're likely buying and why it matters in the evening.

## Wrapping up

Pick a plan that fits your household size and real activities, not the biggest number in the brochure. Mid-tier speeds cover most families; upload speed matters more than it used to; and if your internet feels slow, test on a cable before you pay for an upgrade.

The best plan is one you don't have to think about. If you're constantly aware of your speed, the fix is usually Wi-Fi or router settings — not a bigger number on your bill.
