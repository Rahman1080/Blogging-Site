# How to Identify an AI Scam Message (Text, Email, and Voice)

For years, scam messages had a tell: bad grammar. "Dear costumer, we have suspend your account." It was easy to spot, easy to laugh off. That era is over. Scammers now use AI to write messages that read exactly like a real person — or like a real bank — with perfect spelling, natural tone, and zero suspicious phrasing.

That doesn't mean scams are undetectable. The grammar test is gone, but the structure of a scam hasn't changed. Here's how AI-written scam messages work, the patterns to look for, and how to verify a suspicious message before you trust it.

## What's actually new with AI scams

Two things changed. First, the writing: AI language models can produce convincing messages in seconds, impersonating your bank, your boss, a delivery service, even a loved one, in any tone they want. Second, the volume: generating a thousand personalized scam messages by hand took hours; AI does it instantly.

There's a third wrinkle worth knowing. AI can also generate realistic voices, which is how the same technique moved into phone calls — a "relative in trouble" voice call that isn't your relative. If you want the full picture on AI voice and video scams, our [deepfake scam guide](/articles/ai-scams-deepfake-calls) covers that side. This article is about the written messages: texts and emails.

## The telltale patterns of AI scam messages

Because the writing quality is now too good to judge, look at what the message *does* — its structure and demands. AI-written scams reliably share these patterns:

### 1. It came to you out of nowhere

Scams are unsolicited by definition. A message you weren't expecting — from a "bank" you don't use, a "package" you didn't order, a "relative" who never texts you — is already suspicious. AI can write it well, but it can't make the contact expected.

### 2. Generic urgency

"Your account will be suspended in 24 hours." "Reply immediately to confirm." "This offer expires tonight." AI is excellent at generating urgency, and scammers prompt it to include one. Real institutions rarely push you to act in minutes; scammers always do.

### 3. A request that funnels to one action

Read the message and ask: what does it want? Almost every scam resolves to a single ask — click this link, download this file, reply with a code, pay this amount. A legitimate message from a real company usually offers multiple ways to proceed and doesn't demand one specific action.

### 4. The details are just slightly off

AI impersonation is convincing but not perfect. The "bank" message might use the right tone but the wrong logo, or a support email one character different from the real one. The message might reference a product or service you don't actually have. Check the specifics, not just the polish.

### 5. The link and the destination don't match

This rule survived the AI revolution intact. Hover over links and check where they actually lead. An AI-written message can say "track your package" convincingly while the link points to a lookalike domain designed to steal your login. If the destination doesn't match the text, the message is a scam no matter how well it reads.

### 6. It asks for something a real company wouldn't

Your password, your verification code, your full card number, a gift card purchase, "confirmation" of your login details. No legitimate company asks for these in a message. This single test catches nearly every scam, AI-written or not.

## An example: before and after AI

The same phishing email, old style and new:

**Before AI:**
> "Dear customer, your Paypal acount has been limited. Please verify your acount to avoid suspension. click here: http://paypal-verify.xyz"

**After AI:**
> "Hello Sarah, We noticed unusual activity on your PayPal account and have temporarily limited it as a precaution. To lift the restriction, please confirm your recent transactions through our secure portal within 48 hours: http://paypal-security-check.net/login. If you need assistance, our support team is available around the clock."

Same scam, different wrapper. The AI version has no typos, uses your name, sounds helpful. But the structure is identical: unsolicited, urgent, one action demanded, and a link that doesn't belong to PayPal. Judging the writing got harder; judging the *request* didn't.

## How to verify a suspicious message

When a message feels off, slow down and verify through a channel the scammer doesn't control.

1. **Don't reply, click, or call any number in the message.** Every interaction confirms your number or address is active, which invites more scams.
2. **Go to the source yourself.** If it claims to be from a company, open its app or website directly (not via the link) and check your account or message center there. If it claims to be from a person you know, call them on a number you already have.
3. **Search the exact wording.** Scam templates — even AI ones, especially popular ones — get used thousands of times. Putting a distinctive sentence in quotes in a search engine often surfaces reports from other targets.
4. **Check the sender address and link destinations**, as above.
5. **When in doubt, delete it.** A missed legitimate message costs you a minute; a clicked scam can cost you an account.

## What this means for spotting all scams

Here's the bigger lesson: scams are now well-written, so the reliable defenses are the structural ones. The same checklist that catches [phishing emails](/articles/how-to-spot-phishing-email) — check the sender, hover the links, refuse urgency, never share a code — is exactly what catches AI-written scams. Nothing about AI changed the fundamentals; it only removed the easy signals.

## Wrapping up

AI scams look and read like the real thing, and that's the point. When you can no longer rely on grammar and tone, judge the request instead: unsolicited contact, urgency, a single demanded action, a mismatched link, a request for something sensitive. Verify through a channel you trust, and when in doubt, delete. The message may be generated by a machine, but your skepticism shouldn't be.
