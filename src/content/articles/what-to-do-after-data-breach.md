# Your Account Was in a Data Breach. Now What?

You open an email from a company you used years ago: "We're writing to inform you of a data security incident." Somewhere in the jargon it admits your email address, maybe your password, may have been involved. Now what?

First, the calming news: a data breach notification is not the same as being hacked. It means the company's records leaked — not that someone is in your account right now. Whether it becomes a problem depends almost entirely on what you do in the next hour. Here's the playbook.

Second, a quick reality check about scale. Big, famous breaches dominate the news, but the notification you got might be from a smaller company — a forum you joined once, a store you used a few years ago, a fitness app. The size of the company doesn't change what you should do; the type of data exposed does. And one breach is rarely an isolated event, which is why step five — watching for follow-up scams — matters as much as changing passwords.

## Step 1: Find out exactly what leaked

Not all breaches are created equal. A leaked email address is a nuisance; a leaked password or card number is a real problem. Your notification should say what was exposed — check it for the words "password," "payment," or "bank."

Then check whether the same information appeared in other breaches too. Breach-checking services let you enter an email address and see which known leaks it shows up in; Have I Been Pwned is the most widely used free one, and it also offers alerts so you hear about future breaches faster. A quick caveat: these sites only list breaches that were reported, so a small company's silent leak won't show up. Treat the check as a starting point, not a clean bill of health.

## Step 2: Change the right password first

If there's a chance a password leaked, change it now — starting with the account that was breached, and any account that uses the same password. Even if the notification claims passwords were "encrypted," treat the old one as compromised.

The order matters:

1. **Email first.** Your email is the reset key for almost everything else. If an attacker controls it, they can lock you out of the rest while you're trying to fix things.
2. **Banking and payments next.** Anywhere money moves.
3. **Then everything sharing that password.** The breach only becomes a disaster when one password unlocks many accounts.

If you're making up a fresh password on the spot, use a long one, and don't reuse it. This is exactly the situation a [password manager](/articles/password-manager-guide) is built for — it generates a unique strong password per site and stores them, so one breach stays one breach.

## Step 3: Turn on two-factor authentication

A stolen password becomes useless the moment the account has a second step. If you don't have two-factor authentication on the breached account — or on your email, which protects everything else — this is the moment to add it. Our [two-factor authentication guide](/articles/two-factor-authentication-explained) walks through setting it up with an authenticator app.

If the breached account itself is one you no longer use, consider the bluntest fix available: close it. Every dormant account you keep is one more place your details sit waiting for the next breach. Deleting accounts you've abandoned takes five minutes each and shrinks your exposure permanently — a surprisingly effective long-term habit after any breach.

## Step 4: Check for signs of actual access

A breach doesn't mean someone used the leaked data, but check anyway:

- **Review recent logins** on the breached account and your email — most services show a list of active sessions and recent devices. Sign out of anything you don't recognize.
- **Look for account changes** you didn't make: password resets, new recovery emails, changed addresses.
- **Check your statements** if payment information leaked — watch for small test charges.

## Step 5: Watch for follow-up scams

Here's the part people don't expect: the breach itself is often just the setup for a second attack. Within days you can expect:

- **Emails claiming to be from the breached company** asking you to "verify your account" or "confirm details." Real companies send one notification; they don't email asking for your password.
- **Messages referencing leaked data** — "I have your password from the XYZ breach" — designed to scare you into paying or clicking.
- **Phone calls or texts** using your leaked information to sound legit.

The rule is the same as always: never click the link in a suspicious message, never share a password or code, and if you need to check your account, go to the company's website directly rather than through the message. The same instincts that catch [phishing emails](/articles/how-to-spot-phishing-email) apply here.

## Step 6: Decide if this changes anything long-term

One breach is an event; being repeatedly in them is a pattern. After you've locked things down, a few permanent habits make the next notification a five-minute task instead of a panic:

- **Unique passwords everywhere**, managed by a password manager.
- **Two-factor authentication on email, banking, and anything you'd hate to lose.**
- **A quick quarterly check** of your accounts' recent logins.
- **Data minimization:** the fewer places holding your information, the fewer breaches can involve you.

One more habit that pays off later: set up breach alerts now, while nothing's urgent. Breach-checking sites that offer email alerts mean the next breach finds you, instead of the other way around. You get one calm notification instead of discovering it in a panic months after the fact.

## Wrapping up

A breach notification is a warning, not a verdict. Check what leaked, change the affected password (email first), turn on two-factor authentication, review your logins, and brace for follow-up scams. Done calmly, it takes about an hour — and it makes the next one easier.
