# How to Speed Up Your Home Internet (Without Changing Plans)

If your internet feels slow but you're paying for a fast plan, you probably aren't getting what you signed up for. Here's the good news: in most homes, the fix has nothing to do with the plan. The speed is there — it's just not reaching your devices.

Before you call your provider or upgrade to a pricier tier, work through these free fixes. Most take five minutes, and together they resolve the majority of slow-internet complaints.

## Check whether it's your internet or your Wi-Fi

The single most useful test also happens to be the cheapest: plug a laptop straight into your router with an Ethernet cable and run a speed test.

- If the wired connection is fast but Wi-Fi is slow, your internet is fine and the problem is your wireless network. That's the problem this guide fixes.
- If the wired connection is also slow, the slowdown is upstream — your plan, your connection type, or your provider. No router tweak will fix that.

This one test tells you where to aim, which stops you from buying new gear to fix the wrong problem.

## Restart your router the right way

Your router has probably been running for weeks or months. Over that time it accumulates stale connections and memory clutter — exactly like a phone that hasn't been rebooted in ages. A proper restart clears all of it.

1. Unplug the router from power.
2. Wait 30 seconds, so it fully powers down.
3. Plug it back in and wait two minutes for it to finish starting.
4. Reconnect and run a speed test.

If your network has been sluggish for a while, do this before anything else. It's surprising how often a slow week is fixed by thirty seconds of patience.

## Move the router to a better spot

Placement is the most underrated factor in home Wi-Fi speed. A router hidden in a corner cabinet or sitting on the floor behind the TV is broadcasting through walls, furniture, and appliances.

The ideal spot:

- **Central** in the home, near where you actually use your devices.
- **Up high** — on a shelf, not the floor.
- **Out in the open**, not inside a cabinet or behind a metal TV.
- **Away** from microwaves, fish tanks, cordless phones, and thick walls.

Even moving a router a few feet can make a visible difference. If yours lives in a closet "because it's ugly," that's likely a big part of the problem.

## Split the bands: use 5 GHz for what matters

Most modern routers broadcast two networks — a 2.4 GHz band and a 5 GHz band. They behave very differently:

- **2.4 GHz** travels farther and through walls, but it's slower and crowded (microwaves, Bluetooth, and every neighbor's router share it).
- **5 GHz** is faster and less congested, but its range is shorter.

Your phone and laptop may be quietly sitting on the slower band even when the faster one is available. Some routers handle the switch automatically; many don't.

To nudge things in the right direction:

1. Log into your router's admin page (the address is on the sticker on the router itself).
2. Check the Wi-Fi settings to see whether the 5 GHz network has its own name (many routers call it something like "Home-5G").
3. Connect your laptop, phone, and TV to the 5 GHz network where possible.
4. Leave smart-home gadgets and anything far from the router on 2.4 GHz — they don't need speed, and they'll stop crowding the fast band.

## Reduce how much the network is juggling

A home with a few phones, two laptops, a TV streaming, a console downloading, and a doorbell camera uploading is a lot of simultaneous traffic for one router. Two practical moves help:

- **Pause the heavy stuff.** A game console pulling down a large update will crush the network for everyone else. Schedule big downloads for when you're asleep.
- **Check for freeloaders.** If your Wi-Fi password is weak or was shared years ago, neighbors may be quietly using your connection. Our guide to [securing your home Wi-Fi network](/articles/how-to-secure-your-home-wifi-network) shows how to spot and remove them.

## Plug the important things in

If there's one device whose speed you truly can't afford to lose — a work laptop during calls, a desktop, a gaming console — run a cable to it. Ethernet is faster, steadier, and immune to interference, so even a bad Wi-Fi day won't touch it. Most routers have a few Ethernet ports on the back that are just sitting there.

## When the plan or the hardware is actually the problem

Free fixes work most of the time, but not always. Two situations deserve honesty:

**Your plan might genuinely be too small.** If a busy household streams on multiple TVs while someone games and someone else works, the plan itself can be the bottleneck — especially in the evening. If your wired speed test is slow at the same hour every night, the congestion is on the provider's side. That's when calling to talk about your plan actually makes sense.

**Your router might be the bottleneck.** A router that's five or more years old can cap out well below what your plan offers, particularly if you recently moved to a faster connection. A newer router is one of the highest-impact hardware upgrades for home speed — but only buy one after the free fixes, because new gear can't fix a placement or congestion problem.

And if your Wi-Fi isn't just slow but also dropping out, that's a different problem with its own [set of fixes](/articles/wifi-not-working-fixes).

## Wrapping up

Slow home internet is usually a delivery problem, not a capacity problem. Restart the router, move it somewhere better, put important devices on 5 GHz, and run a cable to the things that matter. Those four moves cost nothing and fix most homes.

If you've done all that and speed still dies at certain hours, the honest answer is that the bottleneck is upstream — and it's time to talk to your provider. For the rest of your home networking questions, browse the [internet category](/articles/category/internet).
