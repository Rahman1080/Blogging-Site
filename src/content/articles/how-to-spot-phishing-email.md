# How to Spot a Phishing Email (Before You Click)

Phishing is the oldest trick on the internet and still one of the most effective: an email that looks official, asks you to click a link or enter a password, and hopes you don't look too closely. The good news is that almost every phishing email leaves fingerprints. Once you know what to look for, spotting one takes seconds.

Here are the red flags, a worked example, and what to do if you've already clicked something you shouldn't have.

## What phishing actually is

Phishing is a scam where a message pretends to be from a trusted source — your bank, your delivery company, your boss — to get you to hand over a password, a credit card number, or access to a device. The word is a play on "fishing": cast enough convincing bait and someone will bite.

Modern phishing has gotten slicker. Years ago, scam emails were full of typos. Today many are polished, and the most dangerous ones are now [written with AI](/articles/how-to-identify-ai-scam-message), which means the old "it's badly written, so it's fake" test no longer works. You need the structural red flags instead.

It's also worth knowing why phishing works despite all the warnings. It doesn't need to fool everyone — just a small percentage of the people it reaches. A campaign sent to ten thousand addresses only needs a handful of clicks to pay for itself. That's why phishing emails arrive relentlessly, why they impersonate whichever brands you're most likely to trust, and why the defense has to be a fast habit rather than a one-time lesson.

## The red flags to check in every email

Run this mental checklist on any email that asks you to do something — click, log in, download, pay, or confirm. If you hit two or more red flags, treat the email as hostile.

### 1. The sender address doesn't match the name

The display name can read anything — "PayPal", "Amazon Prime", "CEO". Look at the actual address behind it. If it's `paypa1-support@example.net` instead of a real PayPal domain, it's a fake. Hover over the sender name in most email apps and the full address shows up.

### 2. The message creates urgency

"Your account will be suspended in 24 hours." "Unauthorized login detected — verify now." "Final notice." Scammers rush you because a calm reader thinks twice. Real companies rarely threaten you by email, and they never give you a countdown to hand over a password.

### 3. The link and the text don't match

Hover over any link before clicking. The blue text might say `www.bank.com/verify`, but the status bar (or the address it reveals) shows something like `www.bank-secure-verify.net`. Mismatched links are the most reliable single indicator of a phishing email.

### 4. There's an attachment you didn't expect

Invoices, "payroll", "tracking labels", "scanned documents" — attachments are how phishing emails deliver malware. If you weren't expecting a file from this sender, don't open it. Banks and delivery companies don't email you random ZIP files.

### 5. The email asks for sensitive information

Legitimate companies almost never ask for your password, PIN, or full card number in an email. An email that asks for any of those is phishing, full stop.

### 6. Something feels off about tone or details

Wrong name ("Dear customer" when you've been a member for years), a deal that's too good, a reply address that bounces, or a slightly wrong logo. Your gut noticing "this isn't how they write to me" is a legitimate signal, not paranoia.

## A labelled example

Here's a typical phishing email, with the tells marked:

> **From:** "Netflix Billing" <billing@netflix-billing-alerts.co.uk>  ← **Red flag 1: the domain isn't Netflix's**
> **Subject:** Your account has been suspended
> **Body:** "We couldn't process your latest payment. Your account will be deactivated in 24 hours. Click here to update your payment details."  ← **Red flag 2: urgency plus fear**
> Link text: "Update payment details"  →  actual address: `netflix-update-payments.secure-login.net`  ← **Red flag 3: mismatched link**

A real Netflix email comes from a netflix.com address, doesn't threaten same-day suspension over one missed payment, and its links go to netflix.com.

## What to do when you spot one

You don't need to fight it or reply. Just:

1. **Don't click the link or open the attachment.**
2. **Report it** — most email apps have a "Report phishing" or "Report spam" button. It trains the filter for everyone.
3. **Delete it.** Forwarding it to friends "as a warning" spreads the risk; a screenshot is fine if you want to show someone.
4. **If it claims to be a company you use**, log in through your app or a bookmarked site — not the link — and check your account normally. If you're genuinely unsure, call the company using the number on its official site.

## What to do if you already clicked

Mistakes happen fast. If you clicked a link or entered information, here's the order of operations:

1. **Change that password immediately** — and any other account that shares it. Do it from a device you trust, not the email's link.
2. **Turn on two-factor authentication** on the affected account if it isn't already. A [password manager](/articles/password-manager-guide) makes generating a fresh, unique password quick.
3. **If you opened an attachment**, run a security scan on your device and watch for slow performance or pop-ups — early signs your [computer may have picked up malware](/articles/signs-device-hacked).
4. **If you entered payment details**, call your bank or card issuer and tell them you may have handed card details to a scammer.
5. **Watch for follow-up scams.** Having your email address confirmed as "active" often triggers a second wave of scams.

## Wrapping up

Phishing relies on speed and familiarity — a fast click, a trusted name. Take those away by checking the sender address, hovering over links, and refusing to act on urgency, and you'll catch almost every one. And remember the newest twist: the same skills apply to [AI-generated scam calls and deepfakes](/articles/ai-scams-deepfake-calls), which attack the same trust from a different direction.
