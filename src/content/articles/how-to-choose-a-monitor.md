The monitor is the part of your computer you actually look at, and it's also one of the easiest things to overthink. Walk into a store and you're hit with size, resolution, refresh rate, panel type, curvature, ports...

Here's the framework that cuts through it: decide your size by your desk, your resolution by your eyes and budget, your panel by what you do, and your refresh rate by whether you game. Ignore everything else.

## Start with your desk and your work

Before any spec, measure the space and define the job:

- **How much desk do you have?** A huge monitor on a small desk is worse than a well-sized one.
- **What's the main use?** Spreadsheets and documents, design work, coding, gaming, or mostly watching video? Each nudges you toward different choices.

Most people fall into "work and general use," which keeps this simple.

## Size: match it to your desk

Monitor size is measured diagonally, in inches. A few honest reference points:

- **Small (around 24")** — fine for a compact desk, and cheapest. Good for occasional use or tight spaces.
- **Mid-size (around 27")** — the most popular work size. Fits a normal desk, comfortable for all-day use.
- **Large (around 32" and up)** — plenty of screen for documents and side-by-side windows, but it needs a deeper desk and you'll sit further back.

A useful rule: a 27" monitor is the default for most people; go smaller for tight desks, larger if you want two windows side by side without an ultrawide. If you're planning dual monitors, two mid-size screens usually beat one huge one for multitasking.

## Resolution: 1080p, 1440p, or 4K?

Resolution is how many pixels the screen has — more pixels means sharper text and more room to fit windows.

- **1080p (Full HD)** — fine, but on a larger screen text looks softer. Good on smaller monitors and tight budgets.
- **1440p (QHD)** — the sharpness sweet spot for mid-size monitors. Crisp text, comfortable for all-day reading and spreadsheets, and not demanding on older computers.
- **4K** — the sharpest, with the most screen real estate. Great for design, video, and detailed work, but it costs more and needs a capable computer to drive.

The honest guidance: if you stare at text and spreadsheets, 1440p is the best value for most people. Choose 4K if you do detailed creative work or simply want the sharpest image. 1080p is fine for small screens or strict budgets.

## Panel types, explained

The panel type decides how the image looks, especially from angles and for colors:

- **IPS** — the safe default. Good colors and wide viewing angles; a bit slower response, which doesn't matter unless you're a competitive gamer.
- **VA** — deeper blacks and higher contrast, which looks great for movies and games; but viewing angles and color consistency from the side are weaker than IPS.
- **TN** — the old cheap panel: fast, but poor angles and washed-out color. Avoid unless the price is the only thing that matters.

For work, video, and general use, **IPS is the recommendation** for most people. Choose VA if movies and games matter more than working from odd angles.

## Refresh rate: when it matters

Refresh rate is how many times the screen updates per second, in Hz.

- **60 Hz** — standard, perfectly smooth for office work, documents, and video.
- **120/144 Hz and up** — visibly smoother motion, which matters for gaming and for anything with lots of scrolling. Beyond that, most people can't tell the difference.

The honest rule: if you game, a higher refresh rate is a genuine upgrade. If you don't, save the money — a 60 Hz IPS monitor with a good resolution will serve you better than a fast panel with a mediocre image.

## Ports and extras

- **Ports:** make sure the monitor matches your computer — HDMI is the universal standard, and USB-C is the modern pick since it can carry video and often charge a laptop in one cable. DisplayPort is common on desktops. Check that your computer actually has the port the monitor uses.
- **Adjustable stand:** a monitor whose height and tilt you can set is worth paying for — neck strain is a real, daily cost of a fixed stand. If you're using a laptop, see our [guide to connecting a laptop to a TV](/articles/how-to-connect-laptop-to-tv) for the same cable logic applied to a bigger screen.
- **VESA mount:** if you want to wall-mount or use a monitor arm, confirm the monitor has VESA mounting holes.

## A quick decision table

| Your situation | Best fit |
| --- | --- |
| Tight budget / small desk | 24" 1080p, IPS |
| Standard work and office | 27" 1440p, IPS, adjustable stand |
| Design or video work | 27"–32" 4K, IPS, good color |
| Movies and games | 27"–32" VA or IPS, 144 Hz if you game |
| Dual-screen multitasking | Two 24"–27" screens |

## Wrapping up

Choosing a monitor comes down to four questions: how big can my desk take, how sharp do I want it, what am I doing on it, and do I game? Answer those and the choice is nearly automatic — size to your space, resolution to your eyes and budget, IPS for most people, and a fast panel only if you actually game.
