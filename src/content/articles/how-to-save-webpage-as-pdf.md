Saving a web page as a PDF is one of those things everyone assumes needs a special tool — and it doesn't. Every browser has the feature built in, hiding behind its print button. No extensions, no downloads, no account. Open the print dialog, choose "Save as PDF," and you're done.

Here's exactly how it works in every major browser, plus the phone versions and one trick for getting a clean PDF instead of a cluttered one.

## The one trick that works everywhere

The universal method across every desktop browser is the same:

1. Open the page you want to save.
2. Press **Ctrl+P** on Windows or **Cmd+P** on Mac (or click the menu → **Print**).
3. In the print dialog, change the **Destination** or **Printer** to **Save as PDF**.
4. Click **Save** (or **Print**) and choose where to put the file.

That's the whole method. Chrome, Safari, Firefox, and Edge all do exactly this, with only minor wording differences. The page becomes a PDF with its text, images, and most of its layout intact.

## Save a web page as a PDF in Chrome and Edge

Chrome and Edge (both Chromium-based) work identically:

1. Open the page, then press **Ctrl+P** (or **Cmd+P** on a Mac).
2. In the print preview, click the **Destination** dropdown.
3. Select **Save as PDF**.
4. Set the paper size and margins if you like (Portrait usually fits articles best).
5. Click **Save**, pick a location, and name the file.

Both also let you choose the page range if you only want part of a long article, and the preview on the right shows exactly what the PDF will look like before you commit.

## Save a web page as a PDF in Safari

Safari on a Mac:

1. Press **Cmd+P**.
2. In the print dialog, click the **PDF** dropdown button in the bottom-left corner.
3. Choose **Save as PDF**.
4. Pick a location and click **Save**.

Worth knowing: Safari's print dialog skips *background colors and images* by default, which usually makes for a cleaner result on articles. If you want a page's exact visual look — say, a design or a colorful graphic — turn on **Print backgrounds** in the dialog first.

## Save a web page as a PDF in Firefox

Firefox is equally simple:

1. Press **Ctrl+P** (or **Cmd+P** on a Mac).
2. Click **Print** in the dialog to open the full print preview.
3. Choose **Save to PDF** as the destination.
4. Click **Save** and choose where the file goes.

Firefox also gives you a **Simplify Page** option in its print dialog that strips ads, sidebars, and menus before exporting — the browser's built-in version of the reader-mode trick below.

## Save a web page as a PDF on a phone

The same feature exists on mobile, tucked behind share and print menus.

**On iPhone (Safari):**

1. Open the page, tap the **Share** button.
2. Tap **Print**.
3. On the print preview, **pinch outward** with two fingers to zoom it into a preview page.
4. Tap the **Share** icon on that preview and choose **Save to Files** (as PDF).

**On Android (Chrome):**

1. Open the page, tap the **three-dot menu**.
2. Tap **Share** or **Print**.
3. Choose **Save as PDF** from the destination options, then tap the download/save button.

Mobile output is a bit less controllable than desktop, but it's perfect for the common use case: grabbing an article to read on the bus or a flight with no signal.

## Get a cleaner PDF (reader mode first)

Printing a busy page gives you a busy PDF — ads, menus, and "related stories" boxes all captured. The fix is reader mode, and it's built into most browsers:

1. Open the article.
2. Turn on **Reader Mode** — the page-icon button in the address bar in Safari and Firefox, or the reading-mode option in Edge. Chrome on desktop lacks a permanent one, but many pages offer a "print-friendly" version, and Firefox's **Simplify Page** in the print dialog is the next best thing.
3. *Then* use the print-and-save steps above.

Reader mode drops everything except the article text and images, so your PDF looks like a clean document instead of a screenshot of a website.

## What the PDF keeps and loses

Useful to know before you rely on it:

- **Keeps:** text, images, most layout, and clickable links (footnotes and URLs usually stay tappable).
- **Loses:** video and animation (you'll get a still frame or a blank space), some dynamic page elements, and anything the page only shows after scrolling or interaction.

If the page is a video or an app-like tool, a PDF isn't the right format — a bookmark is. But for articles, receipts, and reference pages, it's perfect. And if you're saving pages to build research for a project, the same clean-output trick applies whether you're [starting a blog](/articles/how-to-start-a-blog) or just keeping a personal reference library.

## A note on scale and margins

A saved web page can come out cramped, or worse, get cut off at the edge of the page. The print dialog in every browser gives you a little control before you save:

- **Scale:** most browsers default to fitting the page width. If text looks tiny, bump the scale up (or turn off "Fit to page" in Chrome) and it reflows to the paper width.
- **Margins:** Default margins usually look fine. If the output feels heavy on the left, drop the margins to "None" — the PDF still saves cleanly, just with more usable space.
- **Headers and footers:** Chrome and Edge add a page title and URL at the top of every printed page. You can toggle "Headers and footers" off in the dialog if you want a document that looks like a document.

None of these affect the content — they just decide how the page sits on the paper. A quick glance at the preview on the right of the dialog tells you whether it's right before you click save.

## Wrapping up

"Saving a webpage as a PDF" is really just "print it, but to a file." Every desktop browser has the option in its print dialog, phones have it buried in their share menus, and reader mode makes the result look like a document instead of a screenshot.
