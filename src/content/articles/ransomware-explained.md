# Ransomware Explained: How It Works and How to Avoid It

Ransomware is the crime that makes headlines: a hospital, a school, a small business gets locked out of its own files, and a message on the screen demands payment in exchange for a decryption key. It sounds like something from a movie. In practice it's disturbingly simple, and it can hit an ordinary home computer just as easily as a corporation.

The good news is that the defense is also simple — it just needs to be set up before the attack, not during. Here's how ransomware works, why backups are your real shield, and what to actually do if your files get locked.

## What ransomware is

Ransomware is a type of malware that encrypts the files on your device — photos, documents, everything it can reach — and then demands money to unlock them. The demand is the "ransom," usually payable in cryptocurrency to make it hard to trace. A countdown timer is common: pay within 48 hours, or the price goes up or the files get destroyed.

Two things make it uniquely nasty. First, the encryption is real — your files genuinely are locked, not faked. Second, the criminals have no incentive to help you after you pay. They've already won.

## How the attack chain works

Understanding the steps makes prevention obvious. A typical home-computer ransomware attack looks like this:

1. **The delivery.** The attacker gets the ransomware onto your device. For most victims, this is a phishing email — an attachment that looks like an invoice or a document, or a link to a site that silently downloads the malware. Some strains spread through unpatched software or malicious ads.
2. **The infection.** You open the attachment or click the link, and the ransomware runs. It often waits quietly for a moment rather than striking instantly.
3. **The encryption.** The malware encrypts your files, including files on attached drives and network shares it can reach. Fast strains hit hundreds of thousands of files in minutes.
4. **The demand.** A ransom note appears on your screen, usually as a text file or wallpaper, explaining what happened and how to pay.

Notice where the attack starts: a click. This is why phishing awareness is genuinely the first line of defense — our [guide to spotting phishing emails](/articles/how-to-spot-phishing-email) covers the red flags that would stop most attacks before they begin.

## The only reliable defense: backups

You can install all the antivirus in the world and still get unlucky. The one defense that makes ransomware a non-event is a backup you can restore from. If your files exist somewhere the ransomware can't touch, the demand loses its leverage entirely.

The rules for a ransomware-proof backup are stricter than a casual "back up my phone" routine:

- **Back up to somewhere separate.** An external drive you unplug, or a cloud backup service. A drive that stays connected can be encrypted along with everything else.
- **Keep versions, not just copies.** If a backup only holds one snapshot, an attack can infect it before you notice. Versioned backups keep older, clean copies.
- **Test the restore.** A backup you've never restored from is a hope, not a plan. Do a test restore of a few files, or a whole folder, every so often.
- **Back up the important stuff everywhere.** Phones count too — your photos and contacts are exactly the files a ransomware strain on a connected computer could reach. Our [iPhone backup guide](/articles/backup-iphone-guide) shows what a solid phone backup looks like.

## How to avoid ransomware in the first place

Backups are the safety net; these habits stop the attack from ever starting:

- **Treat email attachments and links with suspicion.** The majority of ransomware arrives by email. If you weren't expecting a file, don't open it. If a message feels off, it probably is.
- **Keep everything updated.** Security patches close the vulnerabilities ransomware exploits. Turn on automatic updates for your operating system, browser, and apps.
- **Don't run as an administrator all day.** On a computer, an everyday account with limited permissions limits what malware can do if it gets in. It's an inconvenience that pays off.
- **Watch your network shares.** Ransomware encrypts whatever it can reach — including other computers on your home network and network drives. Don't leave every folder shared and wide open.
- **Use an ad blocker.** Malicious ads are a real delivery method, and blocking them removes the whole category.

## What to do if you're hit

If the ransom note appears, work through this list. The order matters.

1. **Disconnect the device from the network immediately.** Unplug the Ethernet cable or turn off Wi-Fi. This can stop the malware from spreading to other devices and cloud drives — and it can buy you time while the encryption is still running.
2. **Do not pay.** Experts are unanimous here, and it's not just about principle: paying funds the criminals, doesn't guarantee recovery, and marks you as a payer for future attacks. Skip the ransom note's "customer support" link too — it's a phishing page.
3. **Disconnect any backup drives** so they stay clean.
4. **Check what you can restore.** This is where that tested backup saves you. Restore from the cleanest backup you have — ideally one made before the attack.
5. **Report it.** In many places, ransomware is a crime to report to local authorities, and the more that's known about a strain, the more likely a free decryption tool exists for it.
6. **Change your passwords afterward.** If the attacker had access while malware ran, treat every password on the device as compromised. A [password manager](/articles/password-manager-guide) makes this less painful.

## Wrapping up

Ransomware is scary because it's real: a click, encryption, and a demand. But the entire attack collapses if your files exist safely elsewhere. Keep separate, versioned, tested backups, stay suspicious of email attachments and links, update your software, and you've taken away both the delivery method and the leverage. The best time to set up the backup was before you read this. The second-best time is today.
