# QR Codes Explained: What They Are and How to Scan Them

Those square, maze-like codes are everywhere — restaurant menus, bus stops, product boxes, museum plaques. A QR code is simply a barcode that can hold a lot more information than the striped kind, and your phone can read it with nothing more than its camera.

The practical answer to the most common question is short: point your camera at one and tap the link that appears. Here's what they actually are, how to scan them on any phone, and the one thing worth being careful about.

## What a QR code actually is

"QR" stands for Quick Response, and the name is deserved — they're designed to be scanned fast, at any angle, without perfect alignment. A QR code is a two-dimensional grid of black and white squares that encodes data in a pattern a scanner can decode.

Unlike a classic barcode, which holds a small number, a QR code can store thousands of characters. That means it can carry:

- A web address (the most common use)
- Plain text, like a message or instructions
- Contact details (a vCard)
- A Wi-Fi network name and password
- Payment or ticket information

In practice you'll almost always encounter a QR code holding a URL. The scanner reads it, shows you the address, and you tap to open it. That's the whole interaction — you're just pointing a camera at a link.

## Why they came back

QR codes aren't new — they were invented in the 1990s for tracking car parts. But for years, scanning one meant installing a dedicated app, which killed their usefulness for everyone else.

That changed when phone makers built QR scanning straight into the camera. Now the phone that's always in your pocket can read any code instantly, which is why restaurants, transit systems, and packaging adopted them so quickly. You've probably scanned one within the last week without thinking about it.

## How to scan a QR code on an iPhone

You don't need an app, and you don't need to change a setting on most modern iPhones.

1. Open the **Camera** app.
2. Point it at the QR code so the code sits inside the frame. Keep the phone steady for a second.
3. A yellow notification banner appears at the top of the screen showing the link the code contains.
4. Tap the banner to open it, or swipe up to see more options.

If nothing happens, make sure **Settings → Camera → Scan QR Codes** is turned on. It's enabled by default, but it's the first thing to check if scanning silently fails.

## How to scan a QR code on Android

Android is just as simple, though the exact behavior varies slightly by phone brand.

1. Open the **camera** app.
2. Point it at the code and hold steady until the code is recognized.
3. A link or action popup appears — tap it to open whatever the code contains.

On many phones, you can also scan from the lock screen or a widget without opening the camera app. And on Google's own phones, the camera and the Google search bar (tap the lens icon) both scan codes directly.

If scanning isn't built into your phone's camera, open **Google Lens** or the Google app — both read codes reliably.

## How to scan a QR code from a screenshot or photo

Codes you received in an email, a chat, or as a picture can be scanned without a second phone:

- **On Android:** open the photo in Google Photos and tap the **Lens** button, or open the image in the Google app.
- **On iPhone:** in the Photos app, some codes show an "Open this page" option directly. Otherwise, screenshot the image and scan it with the camera app, or use Google Lens through the Google app.

## Are QR codes safe?

This is the question everyone should ask, because the answer has a nuance worth knowing.

The QR code itself can't infect your phone. It's just data — a URL or a string of text. The risk is entirely in what the code points to. A code you find in the wild could lead to a website that tries to trick you into downloading something, entering credentials, or making a payment.

The good news is that every phone shows you the destination before you tap. So the safety routine is simple:

1. **Look at the URL preview** before tapping. If the address looks random, misspelled, or nothing like the place you'd expect, don't tap.
2. **Be extra wary of codes on stickers** placed over official codes, or codes handed to you by strangers.
3. **Never enter a password or payment card number** on a page you reached through a code you didn't fully trust.

QR codes are how a growing number of scams begin — a fake code on a fake notice can point straight at a convincing fake login page. The same caution applies to the links in text messages and emails, which is exactly why our guide to [spotting AI scam messages](/articles/how-to-identify-ai-scam-message) covers checking links before you tap. And since scammers increasingly use QR-style shortcuts in [AI-generated scam calls and deepfakes](/articles/ai-scams-deepfake-calls), treating every code as a link you should verify is the right default.

## Wrapping up

A QR code is a small, fast, information-dense barcode that your phone's camera reads in about a second. Scan with the built-in camera, check the link before you tap, and you get all the convenience with almost none of the risk.
