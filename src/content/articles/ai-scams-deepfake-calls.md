A few years ago, the "grandparent scam" was easy to spot: a shaky voice on the line claiming to be a relative in trouble. Today, that call might genuinely sound like your son or your boss — because AI can now clone a person's voice from a few seconds of audio, and the tools to do it are cheap and widely available.

This isn't science fiction, and it isn't rare. Law enforcement agencies and consumer groups around the world are warning that AI is making old scams dramatically more convincing. The good news: the scams still follow patterns, and knowing those patterns is enough to protect yourself and your family.

## How AI scams actually work

AI didn't invent these scams — it made them easier and more convincing. The three main versions:

**Voice cloning.** A scammer captures a short sample of someone's voice (from a voicemail, a social media post, or a YouTube video), runs it through a cloning tool, and calls you pretending to be that person. The FBI and the FTC have both flagged "grandparent calls" and "boss needs you to buy gift cards" calls that used this trick.

**Deepfake video.** Less common, but growing. Scammers stitch a person's face onto a video call to impersonate executives, celebrities, or family members — often to push fake investment schemes or to authorize urgent payments.

**AI-generated phishing.** Chatbots that write flawless, personalized messages in perfect English. Gone are the spelling mistakes that used to give phishing away. These messages impersonate your bank, your boss, or a delivery company with unnervingly good grammar and details.

The common thread: AI removes the two things that used to expose scammers — bad language and mechanical voices.

## The warning signs to look for

AI is good, but the *situations* scammers create still follow a tired playbook. Watch for these, regardless of how real the voice sounds:

- **Urgency and secrecy.** "I'm in trouble, don't tell anyone," "act now or you'll lose everything," "stay on the line."
- **A new number.** The call or message comes from a number you don't recognize, or the caller insists you call them back on a specific number.
- **Unusual payment methods.** Gift cards, cryptocurrency, wire transfers, or prepaid cards. Legitimate organizations and family members don't demand those.
- **Requests for personal details.** Asking you to confirm your password, PIN, or security answers "for verification."
- **Slightly off delivery.** Even with cloning, an AI voice can have an unnaturally even tone, odd pauses, or a hint of robotic flatness — especially when the caller is asked to repeat something.

## The verification habit that stops most scams

You don't need to become an expert in spotting deepfakes. You need **one habit**: verify through a separate channel.

- **Call back on a known number.** If someone calls claiming to be your child, your bank, or your company's IT, hang up and call the person or organization back on a number you already have — from their official website or your saved contacts. Not the number they gave you.
- **Use a family safe word.** Agree with your close family on a simple code word. If a panicked call doesn't include it, it's a scam. This one rule protects grandparents, parents, and kids alike.
- **Ask a question only the real person would know.** "What did we have for dinner last Sunday?" is harder than it sounds to answer with a cloned voice and no script.
- **Be skeptical of "urgent" requests for money.** Real emergencies still allow for a call back, a second opinion, and a few minutes to think. Pressure is a red flag, not a reason to comply.

## Hardening your accounts while you're at it

Stronger account security doesn't stop a scam call, but it stops the damage if a scammer gets your details:

- Turn on **two-factor authentication** on your email and bank accounts, preferably with an authenticator app rather than SMS.
- Use a **password manager** so a breach on one site doesn't unlock the rest. Our [password manager guide](/articles/password-manager-guide) covers this, and [passkeys](/articles/passkeys-explained) remove the password-phishing risk entirely.
- Set your social media to **private** and be careful what you post. Voice samples, birthday details, and photos with family are gold to scammers building a fake call.

## If it happened to you

Scams are designed to make you feel stupid, so the first step is knowing that it's not your fault — and that acting fast matters.

1. **Stop and document.** Write down what happened: numbers, amounts, dates.
2. **Call your bank or card issuer** immediately. They can often stop or reverse transfers, and they'll want to know how the scam worked.
3. **Report it.** File a report with the FTC at ReportFraud.ftc.gov. If a deepfake of your voice or face was used, report it to the platform where it appeared and to local police.
4. **Warn your circle.** If the scammer cloned a family member's voice, tell that person to change their voicemail greeting and review what's public on their social profiles.

## Wrapping up

AI has made scammers more convincing, but it hasn't changed the underlying crime: strangers pressuring you for money or information under pressure. Treat every unexpected call about money as guilty until verified, get your family on the same page, and use a safe word. That's the whole strategy — and it works.
