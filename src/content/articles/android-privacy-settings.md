Your Android phone already has the privacy controls you need. They're just buried in Settings, and most phones arrive with them set to "permissive by default" — apps ask once, you tap Allow, and years later a flashlight app still knows where you are.

The good news is that tightening these takes about ten minutes, and you don't need a special "privacy" app to do it. Below are the settings that make the biggest difference, roughly in order of impact. Menu names vary by brand and Android version, so if you can't find the exact label, search for the term in Settings — the search box is your friend here.

## 1. Review app permissions, starting with location

Every app you've ever tapped "Allow" on still holds those permissions. Android gives you a single screen to audit them.

1. Open **Settings → Privacy → Permission manager**. (On some phones it's under Settings → Apps → Permissions, or Settings → Location → App location permission.)
2. Tap through each permission category: **Location**, **Camera**, **Microphone**, **Contacts**, and the rest.
3. For each app, ask: does this app genuinely need this? A photo app needs your camera. A game almost never needs your location.
4. For location specifically, prefer **"While in use"** over **"All the time"** for anything that doesn't need to track you in the background.
5. Choose **Deny** for anything that looks unnecessary.

Android's permission manager is the single highest-impact privacy screen on the phone, because permissions are how apps get to your data in the first place. Denying one at the source is far more effective than hoping an app behaves.

## 2. Turn off Google Location History

Separate from app permissions, Google keeps its own record of where you've been — a private timeline of places you visit, stored with your Google account. It powers features like "your timeline" and some assistant suggestions, but if you don't use those, it's data collection for no benefit.

1. Open **Settings → Location → Location Services → Google Location History**.
2. Turn it off. (Or manage and delete the existing history from your Google account at the same time.)

Turning it off stops new collection. You can also delete what's already stored from the same screen or your Google account settings. It doesn't affect GPS navigation or maps directions — those work fine without Location History.

## 3. Manage the advertising ID

Every Android phone has an advertising ID — a random identifier that ad networks use to build a profile of you across all your apps, then serve personalized ads. You don't have to accept that tracking.

1. Open **Settings → Privacy → Ads**.
2. Choose **Delete advertising ID** (or **Reset advertising ID**, depending on your version).

Deleting it is the stronger choice — the ID is gone, and apps can't track you across apps with it. The honest caveat: you'll still see ads, just generic ones instead of tailored ones. That's the trade most people are happy to make.

## 4. Lock the screen properly

A phone with no screen lock is an open book: anyone who picks it up has your email, messages, photos, and whatever apps are already signed in. A lock screen is the cheapest, most effective privacy protection there is.

1. Open **Settings → Security → Screen lock** (called "Lock screen" on some phones).
2. Set a PIN or password, and add a fingerprint or face unlock if your phone supports it — biometrics are faster than typing a PIN every time, and they don't weaken the lock.
3. While you're there, review what shows on the lock screen: **Settings → Notifications → Lock screen**. Hiding message previews stops strangers from reading your conversations without unlocking.

The habit advice that goes with it: a password manager solves the "I'll forget it" excuse. Our [guide to password managers](/articles/password-manager-guide) explains why one good password protecting everything beats thirty weak ones.

## 5. Run a security check and secure your accounts

Android has a built-in security check that walks through the state of your phone and accounts in one screen.

1. Open **Settings → Security** (or **Security and privacy** on some phones).
2. Look for **Security status**, **Security check**, or a similar option.
3. Review what it flags: screen lock, Find My Device, Play Protect, and any apps with special access.

Two more items worth adding while you're here:

- Turn on **Find My Device** if it's off (Settings → Security → Find My Device) so a lost phone can be located and locked remotely.
- Check which apps have **special access** — especially **Accessibility** and **Display over other apps** (Settings → Apps → Special app access). Accessibility access lets an app read your screen, so only apps you trust should have it.

## 6. Check accessibility and "draw over other apps" access

This is the privacy setting people rarely think about, and it's worth a quick look. Apps with accessibility service access can read the content of your screen — that's how screen readers work, but it's also a favorite trick of malicious apps.

1. Open **Settings → Accessibility** (or Settings → Apps → Special app access → Accessibility access).
2. Review the list. Every app there can observe your screen.
3. Turn off anything you don't recognize or don't use. Legitimate screen readers, password managers, and automation tools you set up yourself are fine.

## Keep it up, not one-and-done

Privacy on Android isn't a single fix — it's a short routine. New apps you install will ask for permissions, and Android versions change where settings live (the "keep-current" caveat: if your phone doesn't show an exact menu above, search for the term). Set a loose habit of revisiting the permission manager and advertising ID every few months.

A phone that's less cluttered with tracking is often a phone that runs better, too. ## Wrapping up

Start with the permission manager and location history — those two cover the bulk of what apps and Google collect about you. Then delete the advertising ID, set a proper screen lock, run the security check, and review special app access. Ten minutes, no apps to install, and you've closed most of the data leaks your phone had open by default.

Remember: permission-by-default doesn't mean consent-by-default. You changed it, so it stays changed — until you install something new and tap Allow again.
